A single grab bar professionally installed in a bathroom typically runs $60–$200, depending on parts and whether the install lands cleanly on a wall stud. DIY install with the right anchors and a drill runs $20–$60 in parts. For a full bathroom safety package (3 bars — shower entry, shower wall, beside toilet), expect $180–$600 installed, or $60–$180 DIY.

## What you're paying for

Three cost components:

1. **The bar itself.** $20–$60 for a standard 12–24 inch grab bar from a major brand (Moen, Delta, Kohler). Decorative or designer finishes (oil-rubbed bronze, brushed gold, integrated towel-bar combos) run $50–$120.
2. **Anchor hardware.** $5–$20 if you need toggle anchors when the install doesn't land on a stud. Standard wood screws into a stud are typically included with the bar.
3. **Labor.** $40–$140 per bar for a handyman, depending on local rates and whether tile drilling is involved.

The DIY saving is real — $45–$140 per bar — but it is only a saving if the bar holds. The three places DIY installs fail are a bar screwed into drywall with ordinary anchors, a bar screwed into a fiberglass shell, and a bar mounted where it looked right rather than where the user actually reaches. If any of those is a risk, the handyman visit is cheap. For who to call, see [who installs grab bars](/senior-home-safety/who-installs-grab-bars).

## Installation cost and method by wall surface

| Surface | How it mounts | DIY difficulty | Hired cost per bar | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Drywall over wood studs | Screws into studs; rated toggle anchors between studs | Easy | $65–$160 | Locate studs with a finder, confirm with a finish nail |
| Ceramic or porcelain tile over cement board | Carbide or diamond bit through the tile, screws into stud or rated anchor behind | Moderate | $90–$210 | Tape the spot, start the hole slowly with no hammer mode, keep the bit wet on porcelain |
| Tile over old mortar bed (pre-1980s) | Same as above, slower; mortar is thick and brittle | Hard | $110–$240 | Older tile cracks; a pro prices in one spare tile |
| Fiberglass or acrylic surround | Long screws through the shell into the studs behind, with a backer or sleeve; or an anchor system rated for surrounds | Hard | $100–$220 | Never screw into the shell alone — it flexes and pulls out |
| Plaster and lath | Screws into studs only; toggles hold poorly in lath | Moderate | $90–$200 | Plaster chips around the hole; use a sharp bit and tape |
| Concrete block or poured wall (basement bath) | Masonry bit and sleeve anchors or wedge anchors | Moderate | $100–$220 | Hammer drill required; anchors must be rated, not plastic |

Two shortcuts exist for the hardest cases. A **mounting plate** (a flanged steel plate that spreads the load across several fasteners) lets a bar go where no stud lands. And for a fiberglass surround where the studs cannot be found, some installers open the wall from the other side to add solid wood blocking, then patch the drywall — about $150–$300 extra, but it makes any future bar a 15-minute job.

## Mounting, structural support, and the load test

A grab bar is only as strong as what it is screwed into. The specification that matters is **250 pounds in any direction** — the ADA figure, and the right floor for a home because a falling adult puts a shock load on the bar far above their body weight.

- **Into a wood stud** is the gold standard: two stainless #10 or #12 screws per flange, at least 1.5 inches into the stud. Most 24-inch bars span two studs at 16-inch spacing when mounted horizontally; a 12-inch or 18-inch bar often reaches only one, so mount it diagonally or vertically to catch a stud, or use a rated anchor at the far end.
- **Between studs**, only a toggle anchor sold and rated for grab bars (WingIts, Cobra, or equivalent) will do. Plastic expansion anchors, butterfly anchors, and drywall screws are towel-bar hardware.
- **Into tile**, the tile is not the structure. Drill through it to reach the stud or cement board behind; the anchor or screw does the holding.
- **Into a fiberglass surround**, the shell carries nothing. Reach the stud behind it, with a sleeve or backer so the screw does not crush the shell, or use an anchor system rated for surrounds.

**The load test is not optional.** After every install, stand safely on the floor and pull on the bar with your full weight, straight out and downward. Any movement, creak, or flex means re-anchoring before the bar is used. Repeat the test once a year — screws in old studs loosen.

## What changes the price

**Stud location.** A bar landing cleanly on two studs is the cheapest install — wood screws, 15 minutes. A bar landing in drywall between studs needs grab-bar-rated toggle anchors, which adds 10–15 minutes per anchor point and $5–$10 in hardware.

**Tile.** Drilling through tile requires the right bit (diamond or carbide tile bit), painters tape on the spot to prevent the bit from wandering, and patience. Average handyman charges $20–$60 more per bar for a tile install vs. drywall.

**Bar finish.** Plain stainless or chrome is the cheapest. Oil-rubbed bronze, brushed gold, white powder-coat run $20–$60 more.

**Length.** 12-inch and 18-inch bars are stocked everywhere. 24-inch and 36-inch are usually special-order, adding $10–$30.

**Tile age and condition.** Older tile (1960s–1970s) is more likely to crack on drill. A pro might quote slightly higher to account for the small chance of needing tile repair.

## When DIY makes sense

- You're comfortable with a drill, a stud finder, and a level.
- The install is into drywall or new (2000s+) ceramic tile.
- You can locate studs reliably.
- You're willing to use toggle anchors rated for grab-bar loads ("WingIts" or equivalent) when studs don't cooperate.

The skill ceiling is real but reasonable. Reading the bar's install instructions, watching one good YouTube install video, and taking your time gets you to a safe install for most homes.

## When to hire

- You're uncomfortable drilling tile, especially older tile that's more brittle.
- You can't locate studs reliably with a basic stud finder.
- The bar location requires routing around plumbing in the wall.
- You want a CAPS-certified contractor for a larger aging-in-place project.
- The bar is for someone with a specific transfer pattern (wheelchair, walker, one-sided weakness) — placement then matters more than the hardware, and a [CAPS contractor or occupational therapist](/senior-home-safety/who-installs-grab-bars) should set the height and angle.

## Avoid this mistake

**Don't use suction-cup "grab bars."** These are hand-rail aids for fully-balanced users — they fail under real load. They're a common cause of injury when a user grabs one during an actual loss-of-balance event.

**Don't use standard drywall anchors.** Plastic drywall anchors and butterfly anchors aren't rated for grab-bar loads. Use grab-bar-rated toggle anchors (WingIts, Cobra, or equivalent) or screw into studs.

**Don't skip the load test.** After install, pull on the bar with full body weight while standing safely. If anything moves or creaks, re-anchor before using.

## What to ask for in writing

If hiring out, the quote should specify:

- Bar brand and model
- Length and finish
- Mounting location (in inches from floor and from wall corner)
- Anchor type (into stud or with rated toggle anchors)
- That the installed bar will support at least 250 pounds of load
