<QuickAnswer>
Removing a storm-downed tree costs **$1,500–$4,500** when it's in the yard, **$3,000–$8,000** when it's on a house, garage, or fence and has to be rigged off in pieces, and **$8,000–$18,000+** when a crane is the only safe way to lift it. Stump grinding ($150–$500) and root-ball removal ($300–$1,000) are almost always separate. The spread between quotes on the *same* tree is routinely 2× to 4× — the highest quotes are pricing your urgency, not the work. Get three bids on day two, itemized, and the range collapses.
</QuickAnswer>

## What a storm tree removal actually costs

These are national ranges for a single mature tree (roughly 50–80 feet, 20–36 inch trunk) downed by wind, from published cost surveys, adjusted for what I saw in my own quotes. Small trees cost less; giant oaks and anything over 80 feet cost more.

| Situation | Typical range | What's driving it |
|---|---|---|
| Tree down in open yard, good truck access | $1,500–$4,500 | Bucking, chipping, hauling; no rigging |
| Tree down in yard, poor access (backyard, fenced, slope) | $2,500–$6,000 | Everything comes out by hand or with a mini-loader |
| Tree on a fence, shed, or vehicle | $2,500–$6,000 | Careful piecing to avoid making the damage worse |
| Tree on the house or garage, rigging (no crane) | $3,000–$8,000 | Climber dismantles it in sections, lowers each on ropes |
| Tree on the house, crane required | $8,000–$18,000+ | Crane rental ($1,500–$5,000/day), operator, certified crew |
| Tree tangled in utility lines | Often $0 for the line portion | Utility clears its lines; you pay for the rest |
| Stump grinding | $150–$500 | Separate line, priced by stump diameter |
| Uprooted root ball removal + fill | $300–$1,000 | Cutting, hauling, backfill |
| Emergency / same-day premium | +50% to +150% | Overtime, equipment scarcity, and your urgency |

The last row is the one that matters. A tree company's normal-day price for a big removal is a known number in every market. Storm pricing multiplies it. Some of that multiplier is real cost. Some of it is the estimator looking at your roof and deciding what you'll pay.

## Where the $4,000-to-$18,000 spread comes from

On my garage the scope was identical across every bid, so none of the spread was scope — it was all pricing. On most storm removals it's a mix of the two. Either way, the spread comes from six things, and every one of them is a question you can ask before you sign.

**1. Crane or no crane.** This is the single biggest swing. A crane turns a two-day rigging job into a half-day lift, but it costs $1,500–$5,000 a day to have on site plus an operator and a certified crew. A crane is genuinely necessary when the tree is too big to piece down safely, when there's no room for a climber to lower sections, or when the roof can't take another load. It is *not* necessary for most trees under 60 feet lying on a garage. If one quote includes a crane and two don't, ask the crane bidder why — and ask the others whether they're confident they can do it without one. Both answers are informative.

**2. What "removal" includes.** Some quotes stop at "tree removed from structure" and some end at bare, raked dirt. The cheap one may leave a 40-foot trunk in your yard that costs another $1,500 to deal with. Ask for these as separate lines: (a) remove from structure, (b) buck and haul trunk, (c) chip and haul brush, (d) stump grind, (e) final cleanup. Now you can compare.

**3. Access.** A tree in the front yard next to the street gets a chipper and a dump trailer parked beside it. The same tree in a fenced back yard behind a pool comes out one wheelbarrow at a time. Poor access adds a day of labor. It's a legitimate charge — but make sure the quote is actually reflecting *your* access and not a default worst case.

**4. Emergency scheduling.** Same-day or next-day removal after a regional storm means the crew works your job instead of the four others waiting. Expect +50% for genuinely urgent work. If your house is tarped and stable and you can wait five days, say so and watch the number come down.

**5. Insurance in the picture.** This one nobody says out loud. When a tree service knows a claim is open, the quote goes up. Sometimes a lot. There's a widespread belief that insurance pays whatever is billed. It doesn't — you pay the deductible, debris removal is capped, and an adjuster will question an $18,000 removal on a tree that three other companies bid at $5,000. You are not helping yourself by letting a contractor inflate a claim, and you're the one who eats the difference when the carrier pays the reasonable number.

**6. Who's bidding.** Door-knockers with a truck and a saw price high and deliver unpredictably. Established local companies with an ISA-certified arborist on staff price consistently because they do this every day and have a reputation to protect. The [tree service hiring guide](/advice/how-to-hire-a-tree-service) covers how to tell them apart in one phone call.

## What a fair storm quote looks like

For a mature tree on a garage or one-story roof, rigging (no crane), good street access, full haul-away, stump left:

| Line | Fair range |
|---|---|
| Rig and remove from structure | $2,000–$4,000 |
| Buck and haul trunk | $500–$1,200 |
| Chip and haul brush | $300–$800 |
| Cleanup | $150–$400 |
| **Total** | **$3,000–$6,500** |

Add $150–$500 for the stump. Add $3,000–$8,000 if a crane is legitimately required. Add 50% if you need it done tomorrow.

If a quote for that job comes in at $12,000 with no crane and no access problem, it's not a different opinion about the work. It's a different opinion about you.

## How to bring the number down

**Wait one day.** The single most effective move. Tarp, stabilize, document, and get quotes on day two from companies that know you're comparing. See [the first-24-hours guide](/emergency-repairs/tree-fell-on-house-first-24-hours) for what that day looks like.

**Get three itemized quotes and say so.** Not "I'm getting quotes" — "I'm getting three, itemized, and deciding Thursday." Estimators price differently when they know the number will be compared line by line.

**Ask for cut-and-leave pricing.** If you have a place to stack wood, letting them leave the trunk in rounds cuts hauling and disposal — often 25–40% of the total.

**Separate the emergency from the removal.** If the roof is open, pay for a fast tarp-and-stabilize visit ($300–$800, often through your insurer's vendor). Then bid the full removal on a normal timeline.

**Ask whether a crane is truly required.** If only one bidder wants one, that's a question, not a conclusion.

**Check the neighbors.** After a regional storm, three neighbors with trees down can sometimes get a crew — and a crane — for a single mobilization. Companies will discount for a full day on one street.

## What to push back on

- **A crane on a tree under 50 feet with room to work.** Ask why.
- **"Removal" with no line items.** You can't compare it and neither can your adjuster.
- **A deposit over a few hundred dollars.** Tree work has no materials to order. Some states cap residential deposits by law; California's limit is 10% or $1,000, whichever is less.
- **Any request to sign an assignment of benefits** or to "let us handle the insurance." You pay the contractor; your insurer reimburses you.
- **Pressure to decide today.** The tree will be there tomorrow. The price will usually be lower.
- **No certificate of insurance.** Tree work is one of the most dangerous trades in the country. No workers' comp certificate means an injured climber's claim can land on your homeowners policy.

## Related costs after the tree is gone

The removal is often not the biggest bill. Budget for what the tree hit:

- **Roof repair:** $400–$2,000 for a few punctures and shingles; $5,000–$15,000+ if trusses or decking are broken. Get a roofer up there even if it looked minor from the ground.
- **Fence:** $25–$60 per linear foot to replace a section, plus posts. Fence damage is usually covered under "other structures."
- **Garage door or siding:** $800–$2,500 for a door, $500–$3,000 for siding sections.
- **Gutters:** $6–$14 per linear foot replaced.
- **Landscape and lawn repair:** $300–$1,500 for ruts from equipment and the crater from an uprooted root ball.
- **Arborist assessment of remaining trees:** $150–$450, and worth it. The storm that dropped one tree stressed the ones next to it.
