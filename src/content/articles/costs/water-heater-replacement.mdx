Water heater replacement is one of the most expensive routine-but-unexpected repairs a homeowner faces. The range is wide, and quotes vary by several thousand dollars for the same basic work. Here's what the money actually pays for, and where to push back.

<QuickAnswer>
A standard 50-gallon gas or electric water heater replacement runs **$900–$2,500 installed**, with **about $1,400** the most commonly published figure — tank, labor, permit, haul-away, and code-required items included. Tankless conversion typically runs **$1,500–$4,500** because of larger gas lines, dedicated venting, and often new electrical. Any quote over **$4,000** on a standard like-for-like swap without a clear reason (service upgrade, moved location, gas-line work) warrants a second quote. The **permit is not optional** — skipping it can give an insurer grounds to deny a water-damage claim if the tank later fails.
</QuickAnswer>

<KeyTakeaway title="U.S. pricing">
All figures on this page are in **U.S. dollars** and reflect U.S. plumbing labor rates, code requirements, and energy markets. Canadian readers should treat these as a ballpark only — Canadian tank pricing, gas-fitter rates, and provincial code (e.g., Ontario TSSA gas-permit requirements) differ. Get two local Canadian quotes before budgeting from this page.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What a replacement really costs

A standard 50-gallon gas or electric water heater replacement in 2026 runs **$900–$2,500 installed**, clustering around **$1,400**, including the unit, labor, permit, haul-away, and code-required items. Published national averages sit at $1,333 (HomeAdvisor, Angi) and $1,400 (Fixr); HomeGuide and Fixr both show complex jobs reaching $3,000.

Broken out:

| Component | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 40–50 gal tank heater | $550–$900 | Gas and electric roughly equal at this tier |
| Labor | $300–$900 | 3–5 hours at $75–$150/hr; sources range $150–$1,500 |
| Permit | $50–$200 | Required in most jurisdictions |
| Haul-away | $50–$100 | Often included |
| Expansion tank | $75–$150 | Required in most jurisdictions with check valves |
| Drip pan + drain | $50–$150 | Required by code in many jurisdictions |
| Seismic straps | $30–$80 | Required on West Coast |
| T&P valve discharge routing | $30–$80 | Per code |
| Sediment trap (gas) | $20–$50 | Per gas code |

That's the fully-loaded picture. A quote well under $900 is worth checking line by line — it is usually missing the permit, the haul-away, or a code item an inspector will later flag. A quote over $2,500 without specific upgrades (tankless, moving the location, upsizing, new gas line) warrants a second quote. Between those two numbers you are inside the published national range, and around $1,400 you are at the most commonly reported figure.

## Cost by water heater type

| Type | Unit | Installed (typical) | When it makes sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40-gal gas or electric tank | $450–$800 | $1,200–$2,100 | 1–3 people, like-for-like swap |
| 50-gal gas tank (atmospheric vent) | $550–$900 | $900–$2,500 (~$1,400 typical) | The standard family replacement |
| 50-gal electric tank | $500–$850 | $1,250–$2,200 | No gas service; cheapest to install, costlier to run |
| 50-gal power-vent gas | $900–$1,500 | $1,900–$3,200 | No chimney, or venting through a side wall |
| 75-gal gas tank | $1,100–$1,900 | $2,000–$3,500 | Large households, soaking tubs |
| Heat-pump (hybrid) electric | $1,200–$2,500 | $2,500–$4,500 | Electric homes with basement/garage space; rebates often apply |
| Tankless gas, whole-house | $800–$2,500 | $1,500–$4,500 | End-of-life tank plus a lifestyle reason; needs gas line and venting |
| Tankless electric, whole-house | $500–$1,200 | $1,800–$3,500 | Only with a panel that can carry 100+ amps for it; often needs a service upgrade |

For installed cost, running cost and lifespan side by side, see the [water heater cost comparison](/costs/water-heater-cost-comparison).

Like-for-like is always the cheapest column. Each step away from what is already there — gas to electric, atmospheric to power-vent, tank to tankless — adds venting, gas-line, or electrical work that costs more than the unit itself. For the tank-vs-tankless decision in detail, see [tankless vs tank water heater](/advice/tankless-vs-tank-water-heater).

## Labor, removal, permits, and the extra charges

The unit is usually under half of the bill. Here is where the rest goes, and which lines are negotiable.

| Line item | Typical charge | Negotiable? |
|---|---|---|
| Labor, standard swap (3–5 hrs) | $300–$900 | Ask for flat-rate; compare two quotes |
| Old tank removal and haul-away | $50–$150 (often included) | Should be bundled, not a separate "disposal fee" |
| Permit and inspection | $50–$200 | No — and do not let anyone skip it |
| Expansion tank (code where there is a check valve or PRV) | $125–$300 installed | Required or not; ask which |
| Drip pan with drain line | $50–$150 | Required in most jurisdictions on a finished floor |
| New shutoff valve (ball valve replacing a seized gate valve) | $75–$200 | Worth it if the old valve is stiff — see [the valves near your water heater](/what-is-this/valve-near-water-heater) |
| Flexible supply lines and dielectric fittings | $30–$80 | Usually included; check |
| Gas sediment trap, flex connector | $20–$80 | Code on gas units |
| Seismic straps (West Coast and some other zones) | $30–$120 | Required where applicable |
| Venting changes or new vent | $150–$800 | Only if the new unit or location requires it |
| After-hours or emergency call | +$150–$400 | Avoid by scheduling if the tank is leaking slowly — see [water heater leaking](/advice/water-heater-leaking) |
| Bring-your-own-unit surcharge | +$100–$300 or higher hourly | Price both ways before buying the tank yourself |

Three things to check on every quote: that removal is included and not billed twice, that the permit is in the price with a name on who pulls it, and that each code item is labeled as required or optional. A quote $300 cheaper than another is often missing $500 of these lines.

## Hot water heater vs water heater: same appliance, same price

Half the people who search for this job call it a *hot water heater*; plumbers and the Department of Energy call it a *water heater*. There is no difference in the equipment, the code requirements, or the price. If a quote or an ad says "hot water heater replacement," price it against the ranges on this page exactly as you would any other. The only terms that change the price are the ones that describe the unit: tank vs tankless, gas vs electric, 40 vs 50 vs 75 gallon, and power-vent vs atmospheric.

## Tankless installation

Tankless water heater installation costs more than a tank swap: **$1,500–$4,500 typically, and $5,500+ when the gas line, venting and electrical all need work**. The delta pays for:

- Larger gas line (tankless units fire at 150,000–199,000 BTU vs 40,000 BTU for tank)
- Dedicated combustion venting (PVC or stainless, sealed combustion)
- Often a new dedicated circuit (for powered models)
- Longer labor (6–10 hours vs 3–5)
- The unit itself ($800–$2,500 vs $500–$900 for tank)

Energy savings are real but payback is 5–10 years. If you're replacing a tank that's at end-of-life and you want tankless for lifestyle reasons (infinite hot water, smaller footprint), it's defensible. If you're replacing tank-with-tankless purely for energy savings, the math rarely pays unless gas rates spike.

## What you shouldn't pay more than

</PullQuote>

- Standard 50-gal gas or electric, like-for-like replacement: **$900–$2,500 all-in, ~$1,400 typical.**
- Same job with a minor upgrade (expansion tank code-update, new drip pan, service valve): **up to $2,700.**
- Same job with location change or supply line rerouting: **up to $3,200.**
- Tankless conversion with new venting and gas line: **$3,000–$5,500.**

Anything beyond these ranges should have a specific line item justifying the cost.

## Safety and permit rules

**This is YMYL content.** Water heaters involve:
- Natural gas (combustion, CO risk, explosion risk if leaked)
- 240V electric (shock, arc, fire risk)
- Pressurized water (flood risk, property damage)
- Thermal expansion (pressure rupture risk without expansion tank)

Permits are required in virtually every US jurisdiction for water heater replacement — even like-for-like. The permit and inspection protect you from:
- Insurance coverage denial if the unit fails
- Future resale issues when the home inspector flags the unpermitted install
- Code compliance gaps (seismic, expansion, combustion, venting)

If your installer suggests skipping the permit, get another installer.

To see what the permit itself costs where you live, check the [water heater permit fees by city](/costs/water-heater-permit-cost-by-city) table, which lists the fee each city publishes.

## Getting quotes

Before the first bid arrives, run your own tank type and size through Home Repair Planner's [water heater replacement cost calculator](https://homerepairplanner.com/calculators/water-heater-replacement-cost) so you have a range to hold each quote against.

Ask each quote to specify:

- Make and model of the unit
- Whether the permit is included and who pulls it
- Whether an expansion tank is included
- Whether a drip pan, seismic straps, and code-required items are included
- Whether haul-away is included
- How the labor is billed (flat or hourly with cap)
- Warranty on parts and labor

Compare line-by-line. A quote that seems $300 cheaper may be missing $500 of code items.

Whoever does the install, the new tank will sit on the same floor as the old one. The cheapest thing you can add to the job is a way to find out the day it starts leaking instead of the week after — the [water leak detector guide](/tools/best-water-leak-detectors) covers which type belongs in the drip pan.

## DIY note

We don't recommend DIY water heater replacement in most cases — see our [DIY or Hire verdict](/diy-or-hire/water-heater). The cost savings ($500–$1,000) don't justify the risk-adjusted exposure (permit, insurance, gas, electric, flood). If you do proceed, pull the permit first, follow every code requirement, and call for inspection before covering any connections.

## Energy considerations

Energy efficiency on water heaters has improved meaningfully in the past decade. Modern units carry Uniform Energy Factor (UEF) ratings; higher is better. Heat-pump water heaters (hybrid electric) have UEF ratings in the 2.5–4.0 range vs 0.6–0.7 for standard electric — the electricity bill savings can reach $300+/year. Upfront cost is higher ($1,200–$2,500 for the unit, $2,500–$4,500 installed) but many utilities and the federal IRA offer rebates worth $500–$2,000.

Worth considering if:
- Your existing water heater is electric
- You have space in a basement or garage (heat pumps make noise and cool the air around them)
- Your utility rebate is substantial

Not worth it if you have cheap natural gas and an existing gas hookup.
