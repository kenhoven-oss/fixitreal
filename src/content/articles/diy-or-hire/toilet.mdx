Toilet replacement is the platonic DIY plumbing job. The mechanical work is simple — two bolts, a supply line, and gravity. The failure modes are visible (a drip you can see, not a hidden leak), the tools required are basic, and the typical DIY savings are $150–$300 on a 90-minute afternoon. A plumber's $400–$500 for the same swap isn't 90 minutes of labor — it's a trip charge, a minimum-visit billing block (commonly one or two hours whether the job takes it or not), the truck, insurance, licensing, and the shop's overhead spread across the day's jobs. That's a fair price for what a plumbing business costs to run; it's just a lot to pay for a job that needs no license and no special tools.

Here's how to decide whether to DIY.

## Start with three questions

**1. Any sign the flange or floor under the toilet is bad?**
You can't inspect the closet flange until the old toilet is off — it's underneath the bowl, sealed in wax. What you *can* check now are the warning signs: a toilet that rocks, water or staining at the base, a musty smell, or a floor that gives when you push on it beside the bowl. Any of those means plan for a flange repair or a pro. If there are none, assume the flange is fine, do the real inspection at step 7 below, and know that if it turns out cracked, rotted or sitting well below the finished floor, that's the point to stop and call a plumber — the old toilet is already out, so nothing is lost.

**2. Is the floor around the toilet solid?**
Push gently on the floor around the base of the toilet. Solid = fine. Spongy or flexing = the subfloor has rotted from a prior hidden leak, which is a call-a-pro situation. You'll need to pull the toilet, assess the damage, and potentially do subfloor and tile work.

**3. What's the rough-in?**
Measure from the finished wall (not the baseboard) to the center of the closet bolts. Most homes are 12 inches. Older homes are sometimes 10 or 14. Get this measurement before you shop — buying a 12-inch rough-in toilet for a 10-inch rough-in bathroom is the #1 DIY mistake on this job.

If all three answers are "yes" / normal, this is a clear DIY.

The savings math is cleanest on the basic replacement: DIY for $150 vs hired for $400. A mid-range toilet with soft-close seat: DIY $300 vs hired $500. The delta holds regardless of how fancy the toilet is — you're saving roughly $180–$240 of labor.

## The actual job

The full 12-step procedure, tools and parts list is in the step-by-step block at the top of this page. Budget 90 minutes if you've never done it, 45 if you have. Three things the steps can't say loudly enough:

- **Step 7 is the inspection.** Once the old toilet is off and the wax is scraped, look at the flange properly: cracked ring, broken bolt slots, rust-through on a metal flange, or a top surface more than about a quarter-inch below the finished floor. A broken bolt slot is a $10 repair ring. Anything worse is where a DIYer should stop and call a plumber — you're not out anything, the old toilet was coming off anyway.
- **Bolt caps first, wax ring second.** Set the closet bolts upright in the flange slots and hold them with the plastic keepers (or a dab of putty) before you place the ring, so they don't fall over when the toilet comes down.
- **Nothing gets forced.** Closet bolts and tank bolts are snugged, alternating sides. Porcelain gives no warning before it cracks.

## When to hire instead

**Hire a plumber when:**
- The existing flange is broken, rotted, or below floor level.
- The floor around the toilet is spongy (subfloor damage).
- You have a bidet-seat, smart toilet, or electric toilet that needs an outlet run.
- You're moving the drain location (never DIY).
- You have a concrete slab floor with a compromised flange.
- You've had two back-to-back floods from prior DIY plumbing attempts and your confidence is spent.

**Fair plumber price: $300–$500 for a straightforward like-for-like replacement (you supply or they supply a standard toilet).** Anything over $700 without clear justification — flange replacement, subfloor repair, rough-in conversion — warrants a second quote. ## One pro tip

Before you drop the new toilet onto the wax ring, set it in place loosely — without the wax ring — and do a test-fit. You want to confirm the closet bolts stick up through the holes in the base, that the tank lid has clearance to the wall behind, and that the overall position looks right. Once a wax ring compresses, it's single-use — you don't want to lift the toilet back up to rearrange anything.
