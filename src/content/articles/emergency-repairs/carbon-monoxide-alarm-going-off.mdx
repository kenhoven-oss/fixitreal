Carbon monoxide is the one home emergency where your senses are useless: no smell, no color, no taste, and early symptoms that feel like ordinary tiredness or a coming cold. The alarm is the only warning you get. That's why the response to a sounding CO alarm is non-negotiable, even when everything seems fine.

<QuickAnswer>
When a CO alarm sounds: **get everyone — including pets — outside into fresh air immediately and call 911.** Don't open every window first, don't wander the house looking for a source, don't press the reset button to "see if it does it again." If anyone has a headache, dizziness, nausea, or confusion, tell the dispatcher — that's suspected CO poisoning and may need medical response. The fire department will meter the house for free; **an HVAC combustion inspection ($150–$500) finds the source** if CO is confirmed.
</QuickAnswer>

## The response sequence

1. **Out, now.** Fresh air is the treatment for CO exposure, and it starts working immediately. Get everyone outside or, in severe weather, to a neighbor's.
2. **Head count** — people and pets. Pets often show CO symptoms before people do.
3. **Call 911 from outside.** Report a sounding CO alarm; mention any symptoms. The fire department carries calibrated meters and will tell you definitively whether CO is present and at what level.
4. **Don't re-enter until cleared.** Silence returning to the alarm doesn't mean the source stopped — it can mean the battery died or the gas dispersed temporarily while whatever produced it is still there.
5. **If CO was confirmed, get the source found before the next night.** That means an HVAC tech with a combustion analyzer — see costs below.

<SafetyWarning>
Do not treat "we aired it out and the alarm stopped" as a resolution. CO from a cracked heat exchanger or blocked flue comes back every time the appliance runs — typically at night, when the furnace cycles and everyone is asleep. Fatal CO incidents are overwhelmingly nighttime events. Confirmed CO with no identified source means the fuel-burning appliances stay off until inspected.
</SafetyWarning>

## Alarm, chirp, or end-of-life?

Three different sounds, three different responses:

| Sound | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loud, continuous pattern (typically 4 beeps, pause, repeat) | **CO alarm** | Evacuate + 911, as above |
| One chirp every 30–60 seconds | Low battery | Replace battery today — not this weekend |
| Chirp pattern per label (often 2 chirps or a coded sequence) | Sensor end-of-life | Replace the unit — CO sensors expire after 5–10 years |

Two honest notes on hardware. First, CO detectors have a **printed expiration date** on the back; an expired unit can chirp forever or, worse, fail silent. If yours is past date, [replace it](/tools/best-carbon-monoxide-detectors) — decent units are $20–$50. Second, if your alarms are the builder-grade combos that came with the house, check the date anyway: they were all installed the same week, which means they all expire the same year.

## Where the CO usually comes from

When the fire department confirms CO and the HVAC tech goes looking, these are the usual sources, in rough order of frequency:

- **Furnace problems** — a cracked heat exchanger or failed venting lets combustion gases mix into the air the blower pushes through the house. This is the classic nighttime CO event.
- **Blocked or disconnected flues** — on the furnace or the water heater. Flue pipes get knocked loose in attics and utility rooms, and chimneys get blocked by nests and debris.
- **Vehicles and generators in attached garages** — even with the garage door open, CO migrates into the house. Portable generators cause the most CO deaths of any consumer product; 20 feet from the house, minimum, exhaust pointed away.
- **Fireplaces and wood stoves** — blocked chimney, closed damper, or negative house pressure pulling smoke back down.
- **Gas ovens or ranges used for heat** — never, even in an outage.

## What finding and fixing it costs

- **Fire department metering:** free.
- **Utility response** (if a gas appliance is suspected): free, and they'll red-tag anything unsafe. Same rules as a [gas leak call](/emergency-repairs/gas-smell-in-house).
- **HVAC combustion inspection:** $150–$500 for a proper inspection with a combustion analyzer on the furnace and water heater, plus a venting check. Insist on seeing the analyzer readings, not just "looks fine."
- **Flue or vent repair:** $200–$600 typically.
- **Cracked heat exchanger:** on a furnace past ~12 years, this almost always means [replacement, $4,000–$8,000](/costs/furnace-replacement-cost) — exchanger-only swaps are technically possible but rarely priced sensibly. A tech who condemns your heat exchanger should show you the crack (borescope photo) or the analyzer numbers. "Trust me, it's cracked" with no evidence is one of the oldest pressure-sales moves in HVAC; get a [second opinion](/advice/three-contractor-quotes) before signing anything over $1,000.

<KensTake>
Every CO story that ends badly has the same middle chapter: the alarm went off, nothing seemed wrong, and someone pulled the battery so they could sleep. The alarm triggering before anyone feels sick isn't oversensitivity — it's the entire design intent. Treat the meter reading from the fire department as ground truth, not your own sense that everything's fine. Your senses are the one tool that provably doesn't work on this problem.
</KensTake>
