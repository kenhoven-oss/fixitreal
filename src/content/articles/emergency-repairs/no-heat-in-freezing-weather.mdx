No heat in real cold is two problems at once: restarting the furnace, and protecting the house while it's down. Most people fixate on the first and forget the second — and the second is where the expensive damage happens. A dead furnace is a $300 repair; the [burst pipe](/emergency-repairs/pipe-burst-first-10-minutes) that follows an unprotected freezing night is a $5,000–$50,000 insurance claim.

<QuickAnswer>
**First:** run the five checks below — thermostat, breaker/switch, filter, gas valve, condensate — they restart a meaningful share of "dead" furnaces for free. **If it won't start:** protect the house tonight — drip faucets on exterior walls, open sink cabinets, close off and heat one room safely — then decide between an after-hours HVAC call (**adds $300–$700 versus morning**) and waiting. Indoor temps holding above ~50°F with pipes protected: morning is usually fine. Dropping through the 40s, or vulnerable people in the house: make the night call.
</QuickAnswer>

## Five checks before you call anyone

Run these in order — ten minutes, no tools, and HVAC dispatchers will ask about most of them anyway:

1. **Thermostat:** set to HEAT, setpoint 5° above room temp, fresh batteries if it takes them. A dead thermostat battery is the most embarrassing $200 service call in the trade.
2. **Power:** furnaces have a breaker in the panel AND a light-switch-style shutoff on or near the unit that gets bumped in utility rooms. Check both. If the breaker re-trips, stop — that's a [call-the-pro condition](/advice/breaker-keeps-tripping).
3. **Filter:** a fully clogged filter overheats the furnace and trips its high-limit switch. Pull it; if you can't see light through it, swap it (keep spares) and give the furnace 30 minutes to reset and retry.
4. **Gas:** valve handle on the gas line at the furnace should be parallel to the pipe. Water heater making hot water is a quick confirmation that gas is flowing to the house at all.
5. **Condensate (high-efficiency furnaces):** if yours has white PVC vent pipes, a full condensate trap or a frozen/blocked exterior vent pipe will lock it out. Clear snow and ice from the intake/exhaust pipes outside; empty the trap if you can access it.

Furnace clicks and tries but won't light? That's usually an igniter or flame sensor — our [furnace-not-igniting guide](/advice/furnace-not-igniting) covers what you can safely check before paying anyone.

## Protecting the house tonight

If the furnace stays down and it's freezing outside, this is the part that actually matters:

- **Drip the faucets** — a thin cold-water stream at fixtures on exterior walls and the ones farthest from the water main. Moving water resists freezing, and an open faucet relieves the pressure that bursts pipes.
- **Open cabinet doors** under sinks on exterior walls so room heat reaches the plumbing.
- **Close off one room and heat it.** Pick a room you can seal (towels under doors), preferably not on a corner with two exterior walls, and concentrate people, pets, and a safe heat source there.
- **Know where your main water shutoff is** before you sleep. If you wake to the sound of running water, seconds matter — the [first-10-minutes sequence](/emergency-repairs/pipe-burst-first-10-minutes) starts at that valve.
- **Long outage in severe cold:** shut off the main and drain the lines (open the lowest faucet and the highest) rather than gambling on drips. An empty pipe can't burst.

<SafetyWarning>
The heat sources that kill people in outages are always the same three: **gas ovens run for heat** (carbon monoxide), **generators in garages or near windows** (carbon monoxide — 20 feet from the house, minimum), and **space heaters on extension cords or near bedding** (fire). Space heaters plug directly into the wall, sit on hard floors three feet from anything burnable, and turn off for sleep unless they have tip-over and overheat protection you'd trust your kids to. If a [CO alarm sounds](/emergency-repairs/carbon-monoxide-alarm-going-off) during any of this, everyone goes outside — no exceptions for cold.
</SafetyWarning>

## The money decision: tonight or tomorrow morning

| Factor | Wait for morning | Pay for the night call |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indoor temperature | Holding 50°F+ | Falling through the 40s |
| Occupants | Healthy adults | Infants, elderly, medical needs |
| Pipes | Protected (drips, drained, mild night) | Exposed plumbing, hard freeze forecast |
| Price difference | Standard trip $75–$150 + normal labor | Add $100–$250 trip premium + overtime labor — commonly **$300–$700 more** total |

The honest math: most no-heat nights in a reasonably insulated house are safely waitable, and the same repair costs meaningfully less at 8 AM. Where people get burned is treating the *house* as the fragile thing. The house is fine at 45°F. The pipes, and the people who can't regulate body temperature, are the real constraints — decide based on those.

When the tech does come, the usual no-heat culprits and fair ranges: igniter $150–$350, flame sensor $80–$200, control board $300–$700, inducer motor $300–$800. Anything over ~$1,000 on a furnace past 15 years should trigger the repair-vs-replace conversation with [replacement numbers](/costs/furnace-replacement-cost) in hand — and a [second quote](/advice/three-contractor-quotes) if replacement is being pushed hard on a cold night. Cold-snap no-heat calls are prime time for pressure sales; a furnace that needs a $250 igniter does not need to become a $7,000 install because it's 15° outside.

<KensTake>
Do the prep in October, not during the event: know the main water shutoff, keep two spare filters and thermostat batteries, and test-run the furnace before the first real cold night. Every one of the five checks above is easier to learn at 65° than at 2 AM. And if your furnace is past 15 years, get its likely-failure parts priced now — the worst furnace decisions are made by cold people at midnight.
</KensTake>
