If a tree just came down on your house, garage, fence, or car: **stay away from any part of the tree that could be touching a power line**, **get everyone out of any room under the impact**, and **call 911 only if someone is hurt, a line is down, or the structure is visibly caving**. Then — before a single branch is cut — take photos from every angle, call your insurer's claim line, and cover any roof opening with a tarp. The removal itself is not an emergency once the house is stable. The quotes are where the money is lost, and you'll get better ones tomorrow than tonight.

<SafetyWarning>
A downed line can be live even when it isn't sparking, and a tree can carry that current through wet bark for the full length of the trunk. If any part of the tree is touching a wire, or if you can see a wire down anywhere near it, keep everyone — including pets — at least 35 feet away and call the utility's emergency line before anything else. Do not touch the tree, the fence it's lying on, or a vehicle it's resting against.
</SafetyWarning>

## The first hour

**Minute 1: People, then power.** Get everyone out of any room directly under where the tree landed, and out of the house entirely if the roof is pushed in, a wall is bowed, or you hear the structure moving. Look for wires before you look at the damage — a tree that took a service drop down with it is the single most dangerous thing in this whole scenario.

**Minute 5: Gas.** If the tree hit anywhere near a gas meter or you smell gas, leave the house, don't flip any switches on your way out, and call the gas utility from outside. Trees pull up roots, and roots run near gas lines more often than people expect.

**Minute 10: Photograph everything before anyone touches it.** Wide shots showing the whole tree and how it's lying, close shots of every contact point with the house, garage, fence, and any vehicle, and shots of the trunk base or root ball. Photograph the inside too — ceilings, walls, any daylight, any cracks. This is the evidence your claim runs on, and it disappears the moment a crew starts cutting.

**Minute 15: Call your insurer's 24-hour claim line.** Not your agent — the claim line on the back of the card or in the app. Tell them a tree is on the structure, ask them to open a claim, and ask two specific questions: "What's my tree-removal and debris-removal coverage?" and "Do you want me to use a preferred vendor for emergency mitigation?" Write down the claim number and the adjuster's name.

**Minute 30: Stop the water.** If the roof is opened up and rain is coming or forecast, this is the one thing you should physically do. A tarp over the hole, weighted or screwed down with 1x3 strips, keeps a $2,000 roof repair from becoming a $15,000 interior restoration. Every policy requires you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage — "mitigation" — and covers the cost of doing it. Keep receipts. If the tree is lying across the hole and you can't safely reach it, that's what the insurer's emergency vendor is for.

## Hours 2–24: the calls that decide what this costs

Once nobody is in danger and the water is stopped, slow down. The tree is not going anywhere. What you do in the next day determines whether this is a $4,000 problem or an $18,000 one — and I mean that literally; those were the two ends of the quote spread on my own garage, for identical scope.

<KensTake>
Two months ago a storm put a tree on the roof of my garage. Every estimate I got was for the same scope — get the tree off the garage roof and haul the debris away. Same tree, same roof, same access, same week. The quotes ranged from $4,000 to $18,000. That isn't a disagreement about the work; the work was identical. It's a 4.5× disagreement about what a homeowner with a tree on his garage will pay. The lesson I'd pass on: the crew that shows up in the first 12 hours is quoting your panic, not your tree. Stabilize, tarp, document, and get quotes on day two from people who have to compete.
</KensTake>

**Get three quotes, and tell each one the others exist.** Storm-damage pricing has an emergency premium built into it that can double or triple a normal removal. Some of that is real — crews are working 14-hour days, and crane time is scarce after a big wind event. Most of it is opportunism. Say, out loud, "the house is stable and tarped, I'm getting three bids, I'll decide by Thursday." Watch the numbers move.

**Ask what's included, line by line.** "Remove tree" means nothing. You need: cut and lower off the structure (rigging or crane), buck and haul the trunk, chip and haul the brush, grind the stump (usually separate), and clean up. A low quote that leaves a 30-foot trunk in your yard and a high quote that leaves bare dirt are not the same job. See the [tree removal cost guide](/costs/storm-damage-tree-removal-cost) for what each line should run.

**Verify insurance before anyone climbs.** Tree work is one of the most dangerous trades in the country, and a crew without workers' compensation coverage means an injured climber's lawyer comes to you — the homeowner — next. Ask for a certificate of insurance showing general liability and workers' comp, and call the carrier on the certificate to confirm it's current. A legitimate company sends this in five minutes. How to vet one is [its own article](/advice/how-to-hire-a-tree-service).

**Don't sign anything that assigns your claim.** Some storm-chasing outfits will ask you to sign an "assignment of benefits" so they can bill your insurer directly. That hands them control of your claim. Decline. You pay the tree service; your insurer reimburses you under your coverage.

## What the insurance will and won't pay

The short version — the [full claim guide](/advice/tree-damage-insurance-claim) has the details:

- **Damage to the house, garage, or fence:** covered under standard policies, minus your deductible. A fence is usually "other structures" coverage, typically capped at 10% of your dwelling limit.
- **Removing the tree from the structure:** covered, because it's part of repairing the covered damage.
- **Removing the rest of the tree and debris:** covered but capped, usually $500 to $1,000 per storm across all trees. This is the number that surprises people. The tree hitting your house is the reason your policy pays for removal at all; a tree that falls in the yard and hits nothing is generally on you.
- **A vehicle:** not homeowners at all. That's your auto policy's comprehensive coverage, with its own deductible.
- **The tree itself:** landscaping coverage exists but it's small (often $500 per tree) and usually only for named perils like fire or vandalism — not wind.

Get the adjuster out before the full removal if you can. Photos are good; an adjuster seeing the tree on the structure is better. If the crew has to go first to make the house safe, that's fine — that's what your photos are for.

## The next week

Once the tree is down and hauled, three things are still open:

1. **The roof.** Even if it looked like the tree just brushed it, get a roofer up there. Punctures from limbs are small, easy to miss from the ground, and leak for months.
2. **The stump and roots.** If the tree uprooted, the root plate may have lifted a foundation corner, a driveway slab, a sidewalk, or a sewer lateral. Look for new cracks, doors that bind, or slow drains in the following weeks.
3. **The other trees.** A storm that took one tree down stressed the ones around it. An [ISA-certified arborist](https://www.treesaregood.org/findanarborist) can tell you in an hour which of the survivors are the next to go, and pruning or removing a compromised tree on a calm day costs a fraction of removing it off your roof.

## What not to do

- **Don't let a door-knocker start work.** Anyone who shows up uninvited with a chainsaw the morning after a storm is the highest-cost, lowest-accountability option available to you.
- **Don't pay a large deposit.** Tree removal is a one-day job; there are no materials to order. A deposit request over a few hundred dollars is a red flag, and some states cap deposits by law.
- **Don't cut anything touching the structure.** Ground limbs, fine. Anything under load, no.
- **Don't wait weeks to file.** Most policies require "prompt" notice, and adjusters get harder to schedule the longer a storm event ages.
