Sewage in the house is really two emergencies stacked: a plumbing blockage, and a biohazard spreading across your floor. The order of operations matters — contain first, diagnose second, and only then get anyone with a snake or a shop vac involved. Get the order wrong and you either spread contamination or pay to clear the wrong drain.

<QuickAnswer>
**Stop all water use in the house immediately** — every flush and every shower cycle pushes more sewage up. Keep people and pets away from the spill. Then diagnose: **one fixture backing up = local clog; sewage rising in tubs, showers, or floor drains you weren't using = main line.** For a main line, use the outdoor cleanout cap to relieve the backup if you have one, and expect **$150–$500 for snaking** plus a camera inspection on any repeat. If several neighbors are backed up at once, call the **city sewer emergency line first — their check is free.**
</QuickAnswer>

## First moves, in order

1. **No water down any drain.** Announce it to the household: no flushing, no dishwasher, no laundry, no showers. On a main-line blockage, water from the second floor exits through your first-floor tub.
2. **Keep everyone out of it.** Sewage is Category 3 water — the contaminated kind. Kids and pets don't cross the doorway; you don't cross it without rubber boots and gloves.
3. **Kill power to the area if water is near outlets or appliances** — at the breaker, not by standing in the puddle. Same rule as a [burst pipe](/emergency-repairs/pipe-burst-first-10-minutes).
4. **Find the cleanout.** A capped pipe (usually 3–4", often white or black plastic) outside near the foundation, or in the basement/crawl space. Opening the outdoor cleanout lets the backup discharge into the yard instead of your house — unpleasant, but a yard is disinfectable and drywall isn't. Open it slowly and stand clear; a pressurized line can discharge with force.
5. **Photograph everything before cleanup** — standing water, the high-water line, damaged items. Whether it's an insurance claim or a city-liability claim, documentation from before cleanup is what pays.

## One drain or the whole house? Read the pattern

This 30-second diagnosis determines who you call and what it costs:

| What you see | What it means | Right response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One sink or tub backs up; everything else drains | Local branch clog | DIY plunge/snake or standard [plumber call, $75–$250](/costs/plumber-service-call) |
| Lowest drains (tub, shower, floor drain) back up when other fixtures run | **Main line blockage** | Stop water use; main-line snaking $150–$500 |
| [Toilet bubbles when the shower runs](/advice/why-does-my-toilet-bubble-when-i-shower), drains gurgle | Main line partially blocked — full backup coming | Camera + clear it now, on your schedule, before it's an emergency on its own |
| Multiple houses on the street backed up | **City main blockage** | City sewer emergency line first — free, and liability may be theirs |
| On septic: backup + soggy/odorous drain field | Septic system failure | Septic pumper ($300–$600) first, not a drain snaker |

## What clearing and fixing it costs

- **Snaking the main line:** $150–$500 through a cleanout. National chains often price higher than good local outfits for the identical cable job — see our [Roto-Rooter vs local plumber](/advice/roto-rooter-vs-local-plumber) comparison.
- **Camera inspection:** $150–$500. Skip it on a first-ever clog with an obvious cause (a flushed toy, a grease habit); insist on it for any *repeat* main-line clog, because repeat clogs have a physical reason — roots, a belly, a break — and snaking alone is just renting the same emergency again in six months.
- **Hydro jetting:** $350–$900 for root intrusion or grease-lined pipe.
- **Excavation repairs:** spot repair $3,000–$10,000; full lateral replacement $8,000–$30,000+. **Never** authorize a dig off one contractor's camera run — require the footage with depth/location marks and a [second quote](/advice/three-contractor-quotes). The gap between two bids on identical sewer digs is routinely five figures.
- **Professional cleanup:** $2,000–$10,000+ depending on spread, per water-restoration industry (IICRC) practices — sewage-soaked carpet, pad, and drywall get removed and disposed of, not shampooed. Any restoration outfit proposing to "clean and dry" soaked porous materials in place is a red flag.

<SafetyWarning>
Sewage exposure is a genuine illness risk — gastrointestinal bugs, hepatitis A, skin infections through cuts. Gloves and rubber boots minimum; wash immediately after contact and keep contaminated footwear out of clean rooms. Anything porous the sewage touched — carpet pad, cardboard, upholstery, stuffed animals — is a discard, not a wash. And never run a regular household vacuum on sewage water; a wet/dry shop vac only, emptied to a toilet or the cleanout, not a sink.
</SafetyWarning>

## Keeping it from happening again

- **Root-prone line (older clay/cast iron with trees nearby):** schedule maintenance cabling or jetting annually — $150–$400 planned beats $500 at midnight plus a ruined basement.
- **Grease is the other main-line killer:** it goes in a can, never the sink, no matter how hot the water chaser.
- **"Flushable" wipes aren't** — they're the leading cause of modern main-line clogs. Toilet paper only.
- **Backup-prone basement:** ask a plumber about a backwater valve ($1,500–$4,000 installed) — a one-way flap that physically blocks city-main surcharges from entering your house. In flood-prone areas it's the single best sewage-specific investment there is.
- **Add the sewer-backup rider to your insurance.** Roughly $50–$250 a year against a loss that averages five figures — read your policy tonight while this is fresh.

<KensTake>
The camera-inspection decision is where homeowners lose the most money on sewer lines, in both directions. Skipping the camera on repeat clogs means buying the same $400 emergency every year. But trusting one company's camera run to justify a $20,000 dig is how the sewer-repair industry's worst actors eat. The rule that protects you both ways: repeat clog gets a camera, and any recommended excavation gets the footage handed to you plus an independent second bid before a shovel touches dirt.
</KensTake>
