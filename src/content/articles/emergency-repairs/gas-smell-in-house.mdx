If you smell gas — that rotten-egg, sulfur smell — the rule is simple: **nothing that can spark, everyone out, call from outside.** Natural gas needs only a small ignition source at the right concentration: a light switch, a phone, a doorbell, a pilot light, static from a jacket. The house does not need to "fill up with gas" for ignition to be dangerous; a pocket of gas in one room is enough.

<QuickAnswer>
**Strong smell:** do not touch light switches, phones, appliances, or anything electrical. Don't start a car in an attached garage. Get everyone (pets included) out immediately, leaving the door open behind you, and call **911 and your gas utility's emergency line from outside** or from a neighbor's. **Faint smell near one appliance:** shut off that appliance's gas valve if you can do it in seconds, open windows, step outside, and call the utility. The utility responds 24/7 and **the leak check is free.** Never re-enter until they clear the house.
</QuickAnswer>

## The first 60 seconds

**Strong or spreading smell — treat it as an emergency:**

1. **Freeze your hands.** No switches on OR off, no unplugging, no phone calls inside, no garage door opener. Turning a switch *off* sparks just like turning it on.
2. **Get everyone out now.** Walk, don't search the house for the source. Leave the door open on your way out — it vents without you flipping anything.
3. **Move away from the house** — across the street or a neighbor's yard, not the driveway next to your gas meter.
4. **Call 911 first, then your gas utility's emergency number.** Both from outside. The utility number is on your bill and on the meter; if you don't have it, 911 will dispatch and notify them.
5. **Stay out** until the fire department or utility tells you it's clear. Not "the smell seems gone" — cleared.

**Faint smell, clearly from one spot (a stove burner left slightly on, for example):**

1. Turn the burner or appliance valve off.
2. Open windows in that room.
3. Step outside and call the utility anyway if the smell doesn't clear within a few minutes — a "faint smell" you can't explain is a leak until proven otherwise.

<SafetyWarning>
Do not hunt for the leak yourself with a lighter or match — people genuinely do this, and it's how houses explode. And don't use your phone's flashlight inside a strong-smell house; the phone is an ignition risk. If you hear gas **hissing**, or the smell is strong enough to be unpleasant, skip every checking step and just leave.
</SafetyWarning>

## Who fixes it, and what it costs

The utility's job ends at making the situation safe. Here's how the money side actually works:

| Situation | Who handles it | Typical cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leak check, shutoff, red-tagging | Gas utility | **Free** — 24/7 emergency response |
| Leak on utility side of the meter | Gas utility | **Free** — their infrastructure |
| Leaking appliance connector (stove, dryer, water heater) | Licensed plumber or appliance tech | $150–$350 |
| Leak in house gas piping | Licensed plumber (gas-certified) | $250–$800+ depending on access |
| Pressure test + relight after repair | Plumber, then utility relights meter | Often included in repair; test $75–$150 if separate |

Two things to push back on:

- **A red-tagged appliance is not automatically a dead appliance.** Red tag means "unsafe as found" — often that's a $20 flexible connector or a fitting that needs re-doping, not a new furnace. Get the specific reason for the tag in writing before anyone quotes you a replacement.
- **This is licensed-trade work.** Gas line repair is one of the clearest "no handyman" jobs there is — in most states it's illegal without a license, and an uninspected gas repair can void your homeowner's insurance the same way an unpermitted water heater can. See [how to vet the contractor](/advice/vetting-a-contractor) before you hire.

## Where leaks usually are

When the utility tech or plumber goes hunting, the usual suspects, roughly in order:

- **Flexible appliance connectors** — the corrugated line behind the stove or dryer. Old brass connectors (pre-1980s) are a known failure item and should be replaced on sight.
- **Fittings at the water heater or furnace** — vibration and thermal cycling loosen joints over decades.
- **The stove itself** — a burner valve that doesn't fully close, or a pilot that's out on older ranges.
- **Outside at the meter** — regulator or riser fittings. You may smell this near a foundation vent or basement window rather than at the meter itself.
- **Buried service lines** — rare, but a gas smell *outside* with no obvious source, or bubbles in a puddle over the line, is a call-the-utility-now situation too.

<KensTake>
The free utility leak check is the most underused emergency service in home ownership. People sit on a faint gas smell for days because they don't want to "bother anyone" or they're afraid of a bill. There is no bill. The utility would genuinely rather roll a truck to a false alarm at 2 AM than read about your house in the news. If your nose says gas and you can't prove otherwise in sixty seconds, make the call.
</KensTake>

## After it's fixed

- **Ask the utility tech to relight and check every gas appliance** before they leave — pilots on older water heaters and furnaces don't relight themselves after a shutoff.
- **Add a plug-in explosive-gas detector** in the basement or utility area if the leak was anywhere near the furnace or water heater. Pair it with [working CO detectors](/tools/best-carbon-monoxide-detectors) — a poorly burning gas appliance can produce carbon monoxide long before it ever leaks raw gas.
- **If the leak was an appliance connector, ask the plumber to inspect the others while they're there.** Connectors age together; paying one trip charge to replace three $20 parts beats three separate emergencies.
