Unused openings in an electrical panel rarely look like much, which is exactly why inspectors write them up. "Open knockouts" or "unsealed openings" in a panel mean there is a hole in a box that holds energized parts. The fix is typically cheap and fast. Ignoring it for years is not.

## What the finding refers to

An inspector uses this wording for any of four related conditions:

- **A removed knockout with nothing in it.** Someone knocked out a disc to run a cable, then rerouted or removed that cable, leaving a bare hole in the metal.
- **A cable passing through an opening with no clamp.** A cable that simply sits in a hole can be pulled, chafed by the metal edge, or loosened over time.
- **Empty breaker positions with no filler.** In the front cover, an unused breaker slot should have a filler plate. A missing one exposes the bus bar behind it.
- **Penetrations in a subpanel or outbuilding panel.** These get the same treatment, and they are worth checking in a garage or shed where rodents are more likely.

All of these share one theme. The enclosure is supposed to be effectively closed. The National Electrical Code requires that unused openings in cabinets and boxes be effectively closed, and that cables entering be secured to the enclosure.

## Why it matters

Inside the panel are surfaces energized at about 120 and 240 volts, with significant available current. An unsealed opening can allow:

- Fingers, tools, or a dropped screw to reach live parts.
- Mice, insects, or wasps to nest inside, chew insulation, or bridge conductors.
- Moisture and dust to enter, particularly in a damp basement or detached building.
- Flame or hot gas from an arcing fault to escape the enclosure rather than being contained.

That last point is the engineering reason enclosures exist at all.

## What fixes it

- **Knockout seals.** Inexpensive snap-in or screw-in plugs, sized to the opening. They go in from the inside of the box, so the panel cover (dead front) must come off.
- **Cable clamps or connectors.** A cable that enters needs a listed fitting that secures it and protects the jacket from the sharp edge.
- **Breaker filler plates.** Designed for specific panel brands. Use ones made for your panel. Do not improvise with tape or cardboard.
- **Replacing a corroded or damaged enclosure.** Rare, but possible where water has been getting in.

Removing the dead front exposes live conductors. Even with the main breaker off, the wires feeding the main stay energized from the meter side. That is why box-side work belongs to an electrician. ## Typical cost

- Electrician service call to seal openings and add fillers: often $100 to $300, sometimes included when other panel work is booked. See [electrician service call cost](/costs/electrician-service-call).
- Hardware: a few dollars per plug or filler plate.
- Panel replacement if corrosion or damage is found: often $1,500 to $4,000.

A handyman can place front-cover filler plates in some cases, but check the rate and license rules in your area against [handyman hourly rates](/costs/handyman-hourly-rate).
