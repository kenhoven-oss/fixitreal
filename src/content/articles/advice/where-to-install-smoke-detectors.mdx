Where a smoke alarm sits matters as much as whether it works. A well-placed alarm gives you the minutes that get everyone out; a badly placed one either sounds too late or gets pulled down after the tenth cooking false alarm. The rules are short, and they come straight from NFPA 72 and the residential building code.

<QuickAnswer>
**One alarm inside every bedroom, one outside each sleeping area, and one on every level including the basement.** Mount on the ceiling, or high on the wall with the top 4–12 inches below the ceiling. **Keep alarms at least 10 feet from cooking appliances** and away from bathrooms, windows, ducts, and ceiling fans. On a sloped ceiling, install within 3 feet of the peak but not in the top 4 inches. Most houses need 5–8 alarms total, at [$15–$60 each DIY](/costs/smoke-detector-replacement).
</QuickAnswer>

## The three required locations

**Inside every bedroom.** Most fatal home fires start at night, and a closed bedroom door — which you want closed, because it slows fire spread — also keeps hallway smoke from reaching an alarm out there. The alarm in the room is the one that wakes the person in it.

**Outside each sleeping area.** The hallway that the bedrooms open onto. If bedrooms are split between two ends of the house, that's two hallway alarms.

**On every level.** Including the basement and a finished attic. On a level with no bedrooms, put the alarm in the main living space or at the top of the stairs. In a basement, the ceiling at the bottom of the stairs is the standard spot, since smoke from a basement fire moves up the stairwell first.

That's the NFPA minimum and what the International Residential Code requires in new construction. For a typical three-bedroom, two-story house with a basement, the count comes to six or seven alarms. Long hallways over about 30 feet get a second alarm; large open-plan floors may need an extra one so no point is far from coverage.

## Ceiling or wall, and exactly where

| Spot | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Flat ceiling | Anywhere at least 4 inches from a wall. Center of the room is ideal. |
| Wall | Top of the alarm 4–12 inches below the ceiling. Never lower. |
| Corner where wall meets ceiling | Never — dead-air pocket, smoke arrives last. |
| Sloped or cathedral ceiling | Within 3 feet of the peak measured horizontally, but not in the top 4 inches where hot air pockets. |
| Tray or coffered ceiling | On the highest flat section, or high on the wall of the recess. |
| Near a ceiling fan | At least 3 feet from the blade tips so airflow doesn't sweep smoke past it. |

Smoke rises and spreads across the ceiling, so the ceiling is where it arrives first. The 4-inch buffer from walls and the wall-mount window both exist to keep the alarm out of the still air at the edges.

## Where an alarm does more harm than good

An alarm that false-alarms constantly gets disabled, and a disabled alarm is worse than none because everyone assumes it's working. Keep alarms away from:

- **Cooking appliances** — at least 10 feet. Between 10 and 20 feet, use a photoelectric alarm or one with a hush button; ionization alarms that close to a stove are the number one cause of [alarms going off for no reason](/advice/smoke-detector-going-off-for-no-reason).
- **Bathrooms** — shower steam reads as smoke. Keep alarms 10 feet from a bathroom door where the layout allows.
- **Windows, doors, and HVAC supply registers** — drafts push smoke away from the sensor and delay the alarm. Stay 3 feet from supply vents.
- **Garages** — vehicle exhaust, dust, and temperature swings. A heat detector is the right device for a garage; the alarm just inside the door to the house is the one that matters.
- **Unfinished attics and unheated spaces** — condensation and temperature extremes shorten sensor life and cause nuisance alarms.
- **Furnace rooms** — a heat detector if you want coverage there; smoke alarms belong outside the room.

## Ionization, photoelectric, or both

Ionization alarms react faster to fast-flaming fires; photoelectric alarms react faster to smoldering fires and are far less prone to cooking false alarms. NFPA's recommendation is to have both types in the house, either as separate units or as dual-sensor alarms. In practice: photoelectric anywhere near a kitchen or bathroom, and dual-sensor or ionization elsewhere. ## Carbon monoxide alarms follow different rules

CO is roughly the same density as air, so a CO alarm doesn't need to be on the ceiling. Put one on every level and outside each sleeping area — the same hallway spots as smoke alarms — plus one near any attached garage door and within 10 feet of fuel-burning appliances, but not directly on top of them. Combination smoke/CO units are the easy way to cover both in the hallway locations; see the [CO detector picks](/tools/best-carbon-monoxide-detectors) for standalone options near the furnace and garage.

## Interconnection

In a house built or renovated in the last two decades, the alarms are wired together so that when one sounds, they all sound. That's what makes a basement fire wake someone on the second floor. If your alarms aren't interconnected, wireless-interconnect battery alarms can retrofit it in an afternoon without pulling a single wire — [here's how that works](/advice/interconnected-smoke-alarms).
