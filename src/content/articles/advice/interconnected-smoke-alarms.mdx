A smoke alarm in the basement does nothing for a person asleep on the second floor unless that person can hear it — through a floor, a closed bedroom door, and a running fan. Interconnection solves that: when any one alarm detects smoke, every alarm in the house sounds. It's standard in new homes and, for older homes, one of the cheapest safety upgrades that exists.

<QuickAnswer>
**Interconnected alarms all sound together when one detects smoke.** New construction has required it since the 1990s, done with a third wire between hardwired alarms. **In an older home you don't need to run wire** — wireless-interconnect battery alarms pair with each other over radio, install in an afternoon, and cost about $30–$50 per unit. Within a brand, a bridge model can link wireless units to existing hardwired ones. Whole-house retrofit for a typical home: **$150–$350 DIY**, versus $1,500 or more to have an electrician hardwire it.
</QuickAnswer>

## Why it matters more than any single alarm

Most fatal home fires happen between 11 PM and 7 AM. A closed bedroom door — which you want, because it slows heat and smoke by many minutes — also muffles a hallway alarm badly, and an alarm on another floor is close to inaudible. Interconnection means the alarm in your bedroom is the one that goes off, whatever room the fire started in. That's the difference between the alarm system working as a network and working as a collection of individual beepers.

## How it works

**Hardwired.** Every alarm is connected to house power, and a third conductor — red or yellow in most installations — runs between all of them. When one alarm trips, it energizes that wire and the rest sound. This is what's inside the walls of any home built or substantially renovated since the mid-1990s. Replacing a hardwired alarm keeps the interconnection as long as the new unit is the same brand and plugs into the same harness; [details here](/advice/hardwired-vs-battery-smoke-detectors).

**Wireless.** Battery alarms with a built-in radio pair with each other during setup, usually by holding a button on each. When one alarms, it transmits, and every paired alarm sounds. Range is typically enough for a large house, and the units relay to one another, so distant alarms still hear the signal. The batteries handle both the sensor and the radio; sealed 10-year models are common.

**Hybrid.** Within a brand, a hardwired alarm with a built-in radio acts as a bridge: it sits on the wired interconnect and also talks to the brand's wireless units. Replace one hardwired alarm with the bridge model, pair the wireless alarms, and the whole house — wired and wireless — is one network. This is the right approach for adding coverage to a wired home where the new spots have no wiring.

## When code requires it

- **New homes:** hardwired, interconnected alarms in every bedroom, outside each sleeping area, and on every level. No exceptions.
- **Renovations with a permit:** alarms are generally brought up to the new-construction standard, but where the wiring path would require removing finished walls or ceilings, battery alarms are allowed and wireless interconnection satisfies the interconnect requirement.
- **Existing homes with no permit work:** no requirement to interconnect. Some states and cities layer on requirements at the time of sale; [what sellers typically have to do is here](/home-inspection-repairs/sellers-replace-smoke-detectors-before-closing).

Confirm with your local building department before a renovation; requirements vary by jurisdiction and edition of the code.

## Retrofitting an older home in an afternoon

1. **Count the locations.** Every bedroom, the hallway outside each sleeping area, and every level including the basement. [The placement rules are here](/advice/where-to-install-smoke-detectors). Most houses need five to eight alarms.
2. **Buy one brand's wireless-interconnect alarms for all of them.** Mixing brands doesn't interconnect. Sealed 10-year models cost $10–$20 more per unit and eliminate battery changes.
3. **Pair them on the kitchen table before mounting.** Every brand has a pairing procedure — usually holding a button on the first unit, then on each additional one within a set time. Test the pairing with everything at arm's reach; it's much easier than diagnosing from a ladder.
4. **Mount them.** Two screws or the adhesive base the manufacturer supplies, on the ceiling or high on the wall per the placement rules.
5. **Test the network.** Press test on one alarm; every alarm in the house should sound within a few seconds. Repeat from the farthest unit. If one doesn't respond, re-pair it.

Total: $150–$350 in alarms and a couple of hours. Having an electrician hardwire the same coverage in a finished house — cable through walls, boxes, drywall patching — runs [$200–$400 per location](/costs/smoke-detector-replacement), so $1,500 and up for the same result. The wireless retrofit is the sensible choice for nearly every existing home.

## If you already have hardwired alarms

Your interconnection exists — make sure it works. Press test on one alarm and confirm all of them sound. If some don't, the usual causes are a mixed-brand replacement somewhere in the chain, a disconnected interconnect wire at one unit, or an alarm past its life. When [one goes off and the rest don't](/advice/smoke-detector-going-off-for-no-reason), or the rest go off and you can't find the trigger, start with those three.

To add coverage in a room with no wiring, use the bridge approach: replace one hardwired unit with your brand's radio-equipped hardwired model and pair wireless units to it. Our [smoke detector picks](/tools/best-smoke-detectors-for-homeowners) note which lines offer the bridge.

If you can hear the basement alarm clearly through that door, the job is done and you've fixed the one scenario the old setup was going to miss.
