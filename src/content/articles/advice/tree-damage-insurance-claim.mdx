<QuickAnswer>
Standard homeowners insurance pays to **repair whatever the tree hit** — house, garage, fence, shed — minus your deductible, and to **remove the tree from that structure** as part of the repair. Hauling away the rest of the tree and debris is covered but **capped, usually at $500–$1,000 per storm**. A tree that falls in the yard and hits nothing is generally **not covered**. A **neighbor's tree** on your house is **your claim**. A tree on your **car** is an **auto** claim, not homeowners. Document before anyone cuts, call the claim line within 24 hours, and never sign an assignment of benefits.
</QuickAnswer>

I filed one of these claims two months ago, after a storm put a tree on the roof of my garage. What follows is the set of rules I'd want anyone to have before they make the first phone call — most of which nobody explains until you're already in the middle of it.

## What's covered, what's capped, what's excluded

A standard HO-3 policy — the one most homeowners have — splits a fallen tree into four separate questions, and they get four different answers.

**1. Damage to the house (Coverage A).** Covered, minus your deductible, up to your dwelling limit. Roof, framing, siding, gutters, the ceiling that got wet, the garage if it's attached. Wind is a covered peril on essentially every standard policy. This is the part people assume is covered, and they're right.

**2. Damage to other structures (Coverage B).** A detached garage, shed, or fence is "other structures," covered at a separate limit — typically 10% of your dwelling coverage. On a $300,000 dwelling policy that's $30,000, which is plenty for a fence and usually enough for a garage roof. Same deductible applies; it's one deductible per claim, not one per structure.

**3. Removing the tree.** This is where the surprise lives. Two different things are happening:

- **Getting the tree off the covered structure** is part of repairing that structure. If it costs $6,000 to rig a tree off your garage, that's a repair cost under Coverage A or B, not a separate cap.
- **Debris removal** — hauling the tree away once it's off the house, chipping the brush, dealing with the parts that landed in the yard — is a separate, capped coverage. Most policies allow **$500 to $1,000 per storm** for this, sometimes with a per-tree sub-limit of $500. Some policies extend it to trees that block a driveway or a ramp for a disabled resident, even if they hit nothing.

In practice, the line between "removal from structure" and "debris removal" is where adjusters and tree services disagree. Your job is to get the tree service to itemize so the structural-removal portion is clearly separate from the haul-away. An $8,000 lump-sum invoice invites the adjuster to apply the $1,000 cap to a bigger share of it than they should.

**4. The tree itself.** "Trees, shrubs and plants" coverage exists on most policies — commonly 5% of the dwelling limit in total, $500 to $1,000 per item — but it's almost always limited to named perils: fire, lightning, explosion, riot, aircraft, vehicles, vandalism, theft. **Wind and weight of ice are usually not on that list.** You get paid for what the tree damaged, not for the tree.

<KeyTakeaway>
The tree hitting a covered structure is what unlocks the removal coverage at all. Same tree, same storm, lands six feet to the left in the open yard — most policies pay nothing. If yours hit something, that's the claim; the tree removal rides on it.
</KeyTakeaway>

## The situations that confuse people

**A neighbor's tree fell on my house.** Your claim, your insurer, your deductible. Property insurance follows the property damaged, not the owner of the thing that damaged it. Your carrier may pursue the neighbor's insurer — subrogation — if there's evidence the neighbor knew the tree was dead, diseased, or leaning and did nothing. If that succeeds, you get your deductible back. It usually doesn't succeed, because "the tree looked fine" is a complete defense. Don't let this turn into a feud over the fence line; file with your own carrier and let them chase it if they want to.

**My tree fell on the neighbor's house.** Their claim, their insurer. You're only exposed if they can show you were negligent — a documented dead tree, an arborist's warning you ignored, a prior complaint. If you have one of those in your past, call your own agent, because that's a liability question and your policy's liability coverage is what responds.

**The tree hit my car.** Auto policy, comprehensive coverage, separate deductible. Not homeowners, ever. If you carry liability-only, it's out of pocket.

**The tree fell in the yard and hit nothing.** Usually not covered. Check for a driveway-blocking or ramp-blocking clause. Otherwise this is the one scenario where the $1,500–$4,500 removal bill is fully yours, and where it's worth [getting normal-season quotes](/costs/storm-damage-tree-removal-cost) rather than storm pricing.

**Half the tree is still standing.** Removing the standing half is usually not covered — it didn't damage anything yet. Some adjusters will approve it as mitigation if an arborist documents that it's now hazardous and likely to hit the house. Ask, get it in writing, and don't assume.

**The tree took down the power line to the house.** The utility repairs its line and the connection to the weatherhead at no cost to you. The service mast, meter base, and anything on the house side are yours — and covered as dwelling damage.

## How to file it so it gets paid

**Document before anyone cuts.** Photos and video from every angle, every contact point, the base of the tree, and the interior of any room under the impact. This is the entire evidentiary basis of the claim, and a chainsaw destroys it in an hour. If the crew has to go first to make the house safe, your photos stand in for the adjuster's inspection.

**Call the 24-hour claim line the same day.** Not your agent's office — the claims number on the card or in the app. Say a tree is on the structure, get a claim number, and ask two questions: *What is my debris-removal limit?* and *Do you want me to use a preferred vendor for emergency mitigation?* Write the answers down with the name of the person who gave them.

**Mitigate, and keep every receipt.** Tarping the roof, boarding a window, moving belongings out of a wet room — your policy requires you to prevent further damage, and it pays you back for reasonable costs of doing it. A $400 emergency tarp job is covered. Letting rain fall into the living room for a week because "the adjuster hasn't come yet" is the fastest way to have part of the claim denied.

**Get the tree service to itemize.** Removal from structure, trunk haul, brush chip and haul, stump, cleanup — as separate lines. This is what lets the adjuster apply the debris cap to the debris and not to the whole invoice. It's also what lets you compare three quotes, which is [the only thing that reliably brings the price down](/costs/storm-damage-tree-removal-cost).

**Do not sign an assignment of benefits.** Some storm-chasing contractors ask you to sign a form that lets them deal with the insurer directly and collect the payment. That hands them control of your claim, and it's how homeowners end up with a lien on the house when the contractor and carrier can't agree. You pay the contractor. The insurer pays you.

**Get the adjuster out before the full removal if you can.** Photos are good. An adjuster looking at a tree on the roof is better. If the removal genuinely can't wait, tell the claim line that, and they'll usually authorize it and work from your documentation.

**Ask for the full scope, not just the tree.** The adjuster is estimating the roof, the fence, the gutter, the ceiling stain, the driveway slab the root ball lifted. Walk the property with them and point at everything. What isn't in the estimate isn't in the check, and reopening a closed claim for the gutter you forgot is harder than including it the first time.

<KensTake>
The thing about this claim that I'd put at the top of the list: the debris-removal cap and the tree-removal quotes interact, and nobody tells you that. If the tree service hands you one lump-sum number, the adjuster has to decide how much of it is "removal from structure" (covered as repair) and how much is "debris" (capped at a few hundred dollars). Separate lines on the invoice take that decision away from them. Ask for the cap on your first call, then tell every bidder you need the quote itemized. It's the same discipline I'd use on any vendor invoice at work — make the scope explicit so nobody gets to interpret it against you later.
</KensTake>

## Reading the settlement

**Deductible.** One per claim. If you have a percentage wind/hail deductible — increasingly common in storm-prone states, 1% to 5% of the dwelling limit — a "$1,000 deductible" may actually be $3,000 or $10,000. Look at the declarations page before you decide whether to file.

**ACV vs. replacement cost.** Most dwelling coverage is replacement cost, but the first check is often actual cash value — replacement cost minus depreciation — with the "recoverable depreciation" paid after you show the repair was completed. If the check looks short, that's usually why. Do the repair, send the invoice, collect the rest.

**Code upgrades.** If the roof repair triggers a code requirement — ice-and-water shield, updated fastening — that's covered only if you have "ordinance or law" coverage. Most policies include some; check the amount.

**Disagreements.** If the adjuster's number is well under your contractor's, ask for the estimate in writing, have the contractor respond line by line, and request a re-inspection. If it's still far apart, most policies have an appraisal clause — each side hires an appraiser and they pick an umpire. For a large claim, a licensed public adjuster (paid a percentage of the recovery, typically 5–15%) can be worth it. For a $6,000 claim, it isn't.

## The one-page version

1. Photos and video of everything, before a single cut.
2. Call the claim line the same day. Get the claim number and the debris-removal limit.
3. Tarp and mitigate. Keep receipts.
4. Three itemized tree-removal quotes, structure-removal separate from haul-away.
5. Adjuster on site before the full removal if at all possible.
6. Never sign an assignment of benefits.
7. Walk the adjuster past everything the tree touched, not just the roof.
8. Expect an ACV check first; collect the depreciation after the repair.

This is general information about how standard policies typically work, not advice about your policy. Coverage, limits, and exclusions vary by carrier and state — your declarations page and the policy form control, and your state insurance department's consumer line can help if you and the carrier disagree.
