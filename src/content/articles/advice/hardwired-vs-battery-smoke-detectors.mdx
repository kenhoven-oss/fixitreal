Every smoke alarm in a house is one of two things: wired into the house's electrical system with a battery as backup, or running on a battery alone. Which one you have decides what you can buy to replace it, whether it talks to the other alarms, and whether replacing it is a five-minute job or an electrician visit.

<QuickAnswer>
**Hardwired alarms run on house power with a backup battery and are wired together so they all sound at once.** They're required in homes built or substantially renovated since the 1990s. **Battery alarms run on a battery alone** and are what older homes have and what you add anywhere new wiring isn't practical. Both need replacing every 10 years. Don't swap a hardwired alarm for a battery-only one — replace like with like, matching brand so the interconnect keeps working. Cost: [$15–$60 per unit DIY either way](/costs/smoke-detector-replacement); hardwired units in new locations are electrician work.
</QuickAnswer>

## Telling them apart

Twist the alarm off its base — a quarter-turn counterclockwise. A hardwired alarm has a wiring harness plugged into its back: black (hot), white (neutral), and a third wire, usually red or yellow, that carries the interconnect signal. A battery alarm has a battery compartment and nothing else. On the face, a steady green LED means AC power is present, which means hardwired.

## What each type actually gives you

| | Hardwired | Battery |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Power | House circuit, with backup battery | Battery only (9V, AA, or sealed 10-year lithium) |
| Interconnected | Yes, via the third wire — one alarm triggers all | Only if you buy wireless-interconnect models |
| Works in an outage | Yes, on backup battery | Yes |
| Yearly battery change | Backup battery, unless it's a sealed 10-year unit | Yes, unless sealed 10-year |
| Replacement | Plug the new unit into the existing harness — DIY | Mount and go — DIY |
| Adding one where none exists | Electrician runs cable: $200–$400 per location | $15–$60, five minutes |
| Where required by code | New construction and major renovations | Existing homes; retrofits where wiring isn't accessible |

Interconnection is the real difference. A basement fire at 3 AM sets off the basement alarm; if the alarms are interconnected, it also sets off the one in your bedroom. If they aren't, you're relying on hearing an alarm two floors down through a closed door. That's why code has required hardwired, interconnected alarms in new homes since the early 1990s, and why the answer to "can I just use battery ones" in a house that has hardwired alarms is no.

## Replacing a hardwired alarm

This is easier than most people expect. Turn off the breaker for the alarm circuit, twist the old alarm off, unplug its harness, and plug the harness into the new alarm — if it's the same brand, the connector is identical and the interconnect keeps working. Different brand: the new unit comes with its own harness, and you'll splice three wires with wire nuts inside the box. Mount, restore power, press test, and confirm every alarm in the house sounds. [The full walk-through is here](/diy-or-hire/smoke-detector), and it's a solid DIY for anyone comfortable turning off a breaker.

Mixing brands is where it goes wrong. Interconnect signals aren't standardized across manufacturers, so a new brand in one location may not sound when the others do, or may set them off at random. If your brand is discontinued, replace the whole set together — they were installed at the same time and are all expiring on the same schedule anyway.

## Choosing battery alarms

For any spot without wiring, or in an older home with no hardwired alarms at all, the choice is between a standard battery alarm and a sealed 10-year unit. The sealed unit costs $10–$30 more, never needs a battery change, can't be silenced by pulling the battery, and is done at 10 years like every other alarm. For most homeowners it's the better purchase — the yearly battery change is the step nobody does.

If the house has no interconnection at all, wireless-interconnect battery alarms give you that without any wiring. They pair with each other over radio, and some models pair with existing hardwired units from the same brand. [How to set that up is here](/advice/interconnected-smoke-alarms).

## Which to buy for each location

- **Replacing an existing hardwired alarm:** same brand, hardwired, with a sealed 10-year backup battery. Photoelectric or dual-sensor if it's within 20 feet of a kitchen.
- **Adding an alarm in a house with hardwired ones:** a wireless-interconnect battery alarm from the same brand family, so it joins the existing network.
- **Older home, no wiring anywhere:** sealed 10-year battery alarms throughout, wireless-interconnect if the house has more than one floor.
- **Hallway near bedrooms:** consider a combination smoke/CO unit to cover [both requirements in one spot](/advice/where-to-install-smoke-detectors).

<KensTake>
The question I get most is some version of "the hardwired ones are a pain, can I just put up battery ones." The honest answer is that the hardwired ones are a pain exactly once a decade, for ten minutes per alarm, and in exchange every alarm in the house wakes you up when any one of them trips. Keep them. Buy the sealed-battery version of your brand so the chirping stops being an annual event, and put the money you'd have spent on an electrician toward doing the whole set at once.
</KensTake>
