Walk the outside of almost any older house with a plug-in tester and you will find an outdoor outlet that has no TEST and RESET buttons. A home inspector will usually write it up in nearly these words: *exterior receptacle is not GFCI protected.* This page explains what that means, whether the outlet you have is actually unprotected, and what the fix involves.

## What a GFCI does, and why outdoors is the spot that matters

A ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) watches the current going out on the hot wire and coming back on the neutral. If a small amount leaks somewhere else, such as through a person, it cuts the power in a fraction of a second. A standard outlet has no such sensor, so it will keep supplying power while current passes through someone holding a wet tool.

Outdoor outlets are where that matters most: wet ground, rain, sprinklers, and metal ladders, mowers, and trimmers are all part of the picture. That is why the current National Electrical Code requires GFCI protection for outdoor receptacles, and why inspectors flag an older outlet that predates the rule.

## First, check whether yours is really unprotected

An outdoor outlet with no buttons is not automatically unprotected. A GFCI can protect outlets downstream of it on the same circuit. Before paying for a repair:

1. **Look at the outlet.** TEST and RESET buttons mean the outlet is its own GFCI.
2. **Check the likely upstream spots.** In many houses the outdoor outlet is fed from a garage, basement, or bathroom outlet that is a GFCI. Press TEST on that device; if the outdoor outlet goes dead, it is protected through it. Press RESET to restore power.
3. **Check the panel.** Some houses have GFCI breakers with a TEST button on the breaker itself.
4. **Test it directly.** A plug-in GFCI tester (a few dollars at a hardware store) plugged into the outdoor outlet will trip or not when you press its test button. If it does not trip, the outlet is not protected, whatever it looks like.

If an inspector flagged it and you can find a protecting device that works, tell the inspector or buyer in writing and keep a photo of the test; that often resolves the item without any work.

## The two fixes

**Replace the outdoor outlet with a GFCI receptacle.** This is the simplest fix for a single outlet. A weather-resistant GFCI receptacle goes in the same box. It protects that outlet and anything downstream of it. Our guide to [replacing a GFCI outlet yourself](/diy-or-hire/gfci-outlet) walks through the wiring and the line-versus-load mistake that leaves outlets unprotected. The device runs about $15–$35 for a standard GFCI, and an electrician doing a like-for-like swap runs about $100–$185 installed according to that guide.

**Add protection at the start of the circuit.** If the outdoor outlet is one of several unprotected outlets on a circuit, a GFCI breaker in the panel protects the whole circuit. That is a job for an electrician, because it means working inside the panel.

Either way, outdoor receptacles should be weather-resistant types, and the outlet needs an in-use cover (a hinged cover that closes over a plugged-in cord), not just a flat plate. Inspectors often note both on the same line.

## How much to budget

A single like-for-like swap by an electrician is typically $100–$185 for the visit and device according to our [GFCI outlet cost table](/diy-or-hire/gfci-outlet). Check how the company bills the trip; see our [electrician service-call guide](/costs/electrician-service-call) for what the $80–$200 trip fee does and does not include. Bundle it with any other small electrical items on the report, such as [an open hot outlet](/advice/open-hot-outlet), so you pay one visit instead of two.

## If you are selling or buying

For a seller, this is a cheap, same-day fix that removes a line from the report before the buyer's inspector writes it. For a buyer, it is a small item that is reasonable to ask the seller to correct, or to credit, because it is cheap and it is a safety item rather than a cosmetic one. See our guide to [what must actually be fixed after a home inspection](/advice/home-inspection-what-must-be-fixed) for where an item like this sits in the negotiation.

## When it keeps tripping after you fix it

A new outdoor GFCI that trips repeatedly is usually reacting to moisture in the box, a damaged cord, or a tool with a ground fault, not a defective outlet. See [GFCI outlet keeps tripping](/advice/gfci-outlet-keeps-tripping) for how to narrow it down safely.

## Bottom line

An exterior outlet without GFCI protection is a real but small finding. Test it before you pay anyone, because it may already be protected from another outlet. If it is not, the fix is a GFCI receptacle at the outlet or a GFCI breaker in the panel, both modest jobs, and worth doing before outdoor tools and wet conditions are involved.
