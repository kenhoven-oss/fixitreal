When a home inspector writes that a deck shows rot, sagging, rusted hangers, or an unconfirmed ledger connection, they are describing the structure under your feet, not the paint. Decks fail without much warning, and the cause is almost always water slowly working on connections.

## The parts that matter

A deck is a small structure with a few critical pieces:

- **Ledger.** The board fastened to the house wall that supports the deck's inboard edge. Proper ledgers are attached with through-bolts or structural screws, properly flashed so water does not run behind them.
- **Joists.** The horizontal members under the decking boards. They sit in hangers on the ledger and on a beam at the far side.
- **Joist hangers.** Metal brackets that carry the joist. They need the specified fasteners, not drywall screws or short nails, and must be rated for use with treated lumber.
- **Beam and posts.** The posts carry loads down to footings. Beams carry the joists across the span.
- **Footings.** Concrete below the frost line in cold regions, supporting each post.

## What the typical inspection note means

Here is how the common phrases translate.

**"Rot and sag."** The wood is deteriorating, usually from persistent moisture, and has lost stiffness. A sag in the surface suggests undersized or damaged joists, or settled footings.

**"Rusted hangers."** Corrosion on the metal connectors. Some surface rust on a hanger is expected on an old deck, but section loss, flaking, or bent hangers weaken the connection.

**"No visible lag bolts or through-bolts at the ledger."** The inspector cannot see evidence of a mechanical connection. Nails alone are not an adequate way to hold a ledger to a house. The fastener might be hidden, or it might not exist, and you have to find out.

**"Not properly flashed."** Water runs down the house wall and gets behind the ledger, rotting both the ledger and the house framing.

## How to check your own deck

Take 20 minutes on a dry day.

1. **Walk the surface** and note bounce, soft spots, and any area that dips.
2. **Look underneath** with a flashlight. Inspect the ledger connection for bolts, flashing above it, and staining.
3. **Probe with a screwdriver** at the joist ends, ledger, post bases, and railing posts.
4. **Check the hangers.** Look for rust, bent metal, nails backing out, and joists that have slipped.
5. **Wiggle the railing.** A railing that moves is a fall hazard regardless of the rest of the deck.
6. **Check clearance to soil.** Wood in ground contact stays wet and rots.

Take photos and measure any sag with a string line stretched across the joists.

## What the repair options are

- **Board and railing replacement.** The cheapest category. Decking boards are straightforward to swap.
- **Sistering joists.** A new joist is fastened alongside a damaged one to restore strength when the damage is limited.
- **Hanger replacement.** Remove corroded hangers and install proper ones with the correct fasteners.
- **Ledger repair or reinstall.** Remove the ledger, repair the wall behind it, add flashing, and reattach with structural fasteners. This often requires a permit.
- **Beam, post, or footing work.** Heavier structural repair, sometimes better handled by a rebuild.

The American Wood Council publishes a prescriptive guide on deck construction (DCA 6) that contractors and many building departments reference for fastener spacing and ledger attachment.

## Typical costs

Costs vary a lot by region, size, and deck height. Use these as rough ranges and get written quotes.

- Replacing decking boards or railing sections: often a few hundred dollars up to $1,500.
- Replacing hangers and a few joists: often $500 to $2,500.
- Ledger repair, reflashing, and reattachment: often $1,000 to $4,000, more if house framing is damaged.
- Full rebuild of a typical backyard deck: often $8,000 to $30,000 or more, depending on size, materials, and railings.

## Who to hire

For a deck flagged by an inspector, start with a licensed contractor who builds decks. If the contractor sees evidence of deep structural problems, ask for an engineer's letter. In many areas, ledger and structural repairs need a permit and inspection. Ask first.

## During a sale

A structural note on a deck is a legitimate negotiation point. Ask for a written scope and quote rather than a vague credit, and weigh it with the other items in [what must be fixed after a home inspection](/advice/home-inspection-what-must-be-fixed). The [sample inspection report](/resources/sample-home-inspection-report) shows how deck findings are typically described and prioritized. Sellers can remove many of these notes by repairing hangers and boards before listing.

Seasonal care helps: keep gutters from dumping onto the deck, keep soil and plants away from framing, and clean debris from between boards. Our [winterizing guide](/advice/how-to-winterize-a-house) covers fall prep.
