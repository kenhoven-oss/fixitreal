A three-light outlet tester is a fifteen-dollar gadget every home inspector carries. It plugs into a receptacle and three small lights show a pattern that indicates how the outlet is wired. "Open hot" is one of the fault patterns on its legend, and it shows up regularly on home inspection reports.

## What "open hot" actually means

"Open" means a break in a wire's path. "Hot" is the wire that carries power to the outlet. An open hot means power is not reaching the outlet at all. On a standard three-light tester the sign is simple: **none of the lights come on.** The outlet is dead.

That is different from the two faults it gets confused with:

- **Open neutral:** power reaches the outlet but the return wire is broken. The tester shows one light.
- **Open ground:** the outlet works but has no safety ground. The tester also shows a single light, in a different position.

Light positions vary by brand, so read the legend on your own tester.

## Check these three things first

A dead outlet is not always a wiring fault. Before assuming the worst, rule out the ordinary causes:

1. **A tripped breaker.** Look in the panel for a breaker sitting between on and off, switch it fully off, then on.
2. **A tripped GFCI.** One GFCI outlet often protects several ordinary outlets after it, sometimes in another room, the garage or outside. Press reset on every GFCI you can find.
3. **A wall switch.** Some outlets, or one half of an outlet, are controlled by a wall switch meant for a lamp.

If one of these brings the outlet back and it stays on, there was no open hot. If a breaker or GFCI trips again, that is a separate problem; see [GFCI outlet keeps tripping](/advice/gfci-outlet-keeps-tripping).

## If it is still dead: the usual causes

- **A loose or broken hot connection at that outlet.** Often a wire pushed into the spring-loaded "backstab" hole on the back of the receptacle that has worked loose.
- **A break at an outlet earlier on the circuit.** Outlets are usually wired one after another, so a failed connection at one cuts power to every outlet after it.
- **A worn-out receptacle** whose internal contacts no longer grip.
- **A damaged wire or a failed connection in a junction box**, which is less common and takes more work to find.

The sample [home inspection report](/resources/sample-home-inspection-report) on this site flags three at once: *"Three receptacles located in the dinning room area indicate open 'Hot.' This is a faulty indication. Recommend further inspection, evaluation, and repairs be completed by a licensed electrical contractor."* Several dead outlets in a row usually point to one break upstream, not three separate faults.

## Is an open hot dangerous?

The outlet itself is delivering no power. The danger is in what caused it. A hot wire that is loose or partly broken can arc and heat up at the bad connection, and loose connections are a known cause of electrical fires. Two points matter:

- **Treat the wires as live.** A dead outlet does not mean the box is dead. The break may be right at the receptacle, with a live wire still in the box.
- **An outlet that works on and off is the worse sign.** It means the connection is making and breaking contact, which is when it heats.

If the three checks above do not explain it, turn that circuit's breaker off and leave it off until it is repaired. That is why inspectors send "open hot" to a licensed electrician.

*Corrected on November 22, 2026: an earlier version of this article described an open hot as an outlet that still works and gave a loose neutral as the usual cause. That was wrong. An open hot means no power is reaching the outlet; a broken neutral is a different fault, called an open neutral.*

## How an electrician diagnoses it

An electrician's visit usually goes:

1. Confirm the reading with their own tester and a multimeter.
2. Turn off the circuit at the breaker and remove the affected receptacles.
3. Inspect for backstabbed connections, loose screw terminals, and burned or discolored wire ends.
4. If several outlets on the same circuit are dead, trace the circuit back toward the panel to find the first bad connection, often at the last outlet that still works.
5. Replace the compromised receptacle (a few dollars of parts) and redo the terminations on screw terminals with the wire bent clockwise around the screw.

A straightforward loose-wire fix on one receptacle is usually 20-40 minutes of actual work. Expect to pay an electrician's service-call minimum — see our [electrician service-call cost guide](/costs/electrician-service-call) — typically $150-$350 for the visit and the repair.

If the fault traces to a problem deeper in the circuit — a bad connection in a junction box behind drywall, or a damaged wire in the wall — the repair expands. Opening drywall to reach a junction box and redoing the connections is another $200-$600 with patching, depending on access.

## What about just replacing the outlet myself?

Replacing a receptacle is within the skill range of a careful homeowner who understands that the breaker must be off and verified off with a non-contact tester before touching anything. The failure mode is not that the work is difficult; it is that an incorrectly reinstalled receptacle creates the same loose-connection fire risk the first one had. If you do it yourself:

- Kill the breaker and verify dead with a non-contact tester and a known-live reading (test a known-live outlet first to confirm the tester works).
- Use the screw terminals, not the backstab holes. Wrap the wire clockwise around the screw and tighten firmly.
- Match hot (black) to the brass screw, neutral (white) to the silver, ground (bare/green) to the green.
- Retest with your own three-light tester before closing the box.

If you are uncertain whether you are seeing the complete picture — particularly if the fault appears at multiple outlets, if you see any burn marks or melted insulation, or if your house is pre-1970 with ungrounded cloth wiring — this is an electrician job, not a DIY. The money you save on a service call is not worth guessing wrong about residential wiring.

## When it is not an outlet problem at all

A three-light tester is a screening tool, not a diagnostic tool. With no power at the outlet it cannot report anything else, so a second fault such as an open neutral can be hidden until the hot is restored; retest after the repair. It also cannot detect some wiring errors at all, such as a bootleg ground (a jumper tying neutral to ground to fake a grounded outlet in a two-wire house). Those cases are routine for an electrician and confusing for anyone else. If you remove one outlet and find the wiring looks correct and tight at that device, stop and call a pro rather than opening three more.

## Bottom line

"Open hot" means the outlet has no power. Rule out a tripped breaker, a tripped GFCI and a wall switch first. If none of those explains it, the hot wire is broken somewhere, and that needs an electrician: leave the breaker off until it is fixed. On an inspection report it is a real item to address before closing or soon after, but rarely a dealbreaker. One receptacle is a short visit; several dead outlets on a circuit mean a break upstream, a slightly bigger job that is still routine.
