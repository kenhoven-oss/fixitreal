A three-light outlet tester is a fifteen-dollar gadget every home inspector carries. It plugs into a receptacle, three LEDs light up in a pattern, and the pattern indicates how the outlet is wired. "Open hot" is one of the most-flagged fault patterns on home inspection reports, and it needs to be understood correctly because the label is slightly misleading — the fix usually involves the outlet, sometimes involves a wall, and occasionally involves the panel.

## What "open hot" actually means

Despite the name, "open hot" on a three-light tester most commonly means that the outlet has power on the hot side but something is wrong elsewhere in the receptacle's circuit — most often an issue with the way the device is sensing hot, neutral, or ground. Different testers label the same fault patterns differently, and the common scenarios behind an "open hot" reading include:

- A loose backstabbed wire inside the receptacle box, where a stripped wire was pushed into the spring-loaded hole on the back of the device and worked its way loose.
- Reverse polarity, where hot and neutral were swapped during installation. Some testers call this out separately; cheap ones lump it in with other faults.
- A tester showing the fault at one outlet because the problem is actually upstream — a bad connection at the receptacle feeding it, or at the panel breaker itself.

The sample [home inspection report](/resources/sample-home-inspection-report) on this site flags three of these at once: *"Three receptacles located in the dinning room area indicate open 'Hot.' This is a faulty indication. Recommend further inspection, evaluation, and repairs be completed by a licensed electrical contractor."* When several outlets on the same circuit show the same fault, the problem is almost always upstream of the ones that are reading wrong — a single loose connection at one device is affecting everything downstream.

## Why inspectors flag it as a safety issue

An outlet reading a fault pattern on a three-light tester is not immediately dangerous, but what it indicates can be. A loose connection in a receptacle box is one of the common causes of residential electrical fires — the loose wire arcs, the heat builds, the box or the wall insulation eventually ignites. The National Electrical Code's grounding and bonding requirements exist because small wiring faults can become life-safety problems when something else later goes wrong (a surge, a short, a person touching a metal appliance). That's why inspectors route "open hot" to a licensed electrician rather than the client's cordless screwdriver.

## How an electrician diagnoses it

An electrician's visit usually goes:

1. Confirm the reading with their own tester and a multimeter.
2. Turn off the circuit at the breaker and remove the affected receptacles.
3. Inspect for backstabbed connections, loose screw terminals, and burned or discolored wire ends.
4. If several outlets on the same circuit read the fault, trace the circuit back to the panel to find the first bad connection upstream.
5. Replace the compromised receptacle (a few dollars of parts) and redo the terminations on screw terminals with the wire bent clockwise around the screw.

A straightforward loose-wire fix on one receptacle is usually 20-40 minutes of actual work. Expect to pay an electrician's service-call minimum — see our [electrician service-call cost guide](/costs/electrician-service-call) — typically $150-$350 for the visit and the repair.

If the fault traces to a problem deeper in the circuit — a bad connection in a junction box behind drywall, or a damaged wire in the wall — the repair expands. Opening drywall to reach a junction box and redoing the connections is another $200-$600 with patching, depending on access.

## What about just replacing the outlet myself?

Replacing a receptacle is within the skill range of a careful homeowner who understands that the breaker must be off and verified off with a non-contact tester before touching anything. The failure mode is not that the work is difficult; it is that an incorrectly reinstalled receptacle creates the same loose-connection fire risk the first one had. If you do it yourself:

- Kill the breaker and verify dead with a non-contact tester and a known-live reading (test a known-live outlet first to confirm the tester works).
- Use the screw terminals, not the backstab holes. Wrap the wire clockwise around the screw and tighten firmly.
- Match hot (black) to the brass screw, neutral (white) to the silver, ground (bare/green) to the green.
- Retest with your own three-light tester before closing the box.

If you are uncertain whether you are seeing the complete picture — particularly if the fault appears at multiple outlets, if you see any burn marks or melted insulation, or if your house is pre-1970 with ungrounded cloth wiring — this is an electrician job, not a DIY. The money you save on a service call is not worth guessing wrong about residential wiring.

## When it is not an outlet problem at all

A three-light tester is a screening tool, not a diagnostic tool. Sometimes the actual issue is a bootleg ground (a jumper wire tying neutral to ground to fake a grounded outlet in a two-wire house), a mis-wired GFCI upstream, or a shared neutral on a multi-wire branch circuit that has come loose. Those cases are routine for an electrician and confusing for anyone else. If you remove one outlet and find the wiring looks correct and tight at that device, stop and call a pro rather than opening three more.

## Bottom line

"Open hot" on an inspection report is almost always a real fault worth addressing before closing or shortly after move-in, but it is rarely a dealbreaker. A single receptacle fix is a short electrician visit. Multiple outlets on the same circuit indicate a problem upstream and are a slightly bigger job but still routine. Treat the inspector's recommendation as what it is — a safety-flagged item that an electrician should look at within a reasonable window, not an emergency and not something to patch over with a cosmetic outlet swap.
