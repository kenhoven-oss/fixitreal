Kick-out flashing is one of the most-common "further evaluation" items on a home inspection report and one of the least understood by buyers. It is a tiny piece of metal — usually three to six inches wide — and its absence can cause tens of thousands of dollars in hidden wall damage. The problem isn't the missing part. It is the years of water that quietly ran behind the siding while nobody knew.

## What kick-out flashing actually does

Where a sloped roof ends against a vertical wall — a front porch roof dying into the side of the house, a garage roof meeting the main house wall, a dormer side — the roof's step flashing carries water down along the wall-to-roof joint. At the bottom of that joint, where the roof terminates above a gutter or next to siding, something has to divert the water outward into the gutter. Otherwise it continues down the wall, slips behind the siding, and soaks the wall sheathing and framing underneath.

That diverter is the kick-out. It is a short piece of metal flashing, bent at an angle, installed as the last step of the step-flashing run. It has been required by the International Residential Code at roof-wall intersections where the roof terminates above a wall for over a decade, but there are millions of US homes built before that requirement, and plenty of post-code homes where it was skipped during construction or lost during a reroof.

## Why the damage is hidden

A missing kick-out is invisible from the ground. The siding behind it looks fine from the yard, and the interior drywall shows nothing for years. Then one day a bulge appears, or an interior corner starts to show a water stain, or someone opens the wall for an unrelated reason and finds black rot across several studs. By the time the symptom is visible, the sheathing has been wet for every rainfall for years.

The sample [home inspection report](/resources/sample-home-inspection-report) on this site flags exactly this item, verbatim: *"No kick out flashing is installed on the lower front roof section and the back side of the garage roof. This can provide water direct access into the wall area."* The inspector is doing what inspectors do — identifying a known risk even when the damage underneath is not yet visible.

## How to evaluate it on a specific house

Three questions get you most of the way to a decision:

1. **How long has it been missing?** A new build should have it. A 1990s house might have been built without it. A recent reroof that removed it is a contractor error worth raising with the roofer.
2. **Is there visible evidence of water behind the siding?** Staining on the siding below the joint, bubbled paint, soft trim at the corner, or an interior stain on the matching wall are all flags for existing damage.
3. **What's downstream?** A kick-out that empties into a gutter is one problem. A kick-out that empties onto a lower roof section or against siding below creates a secondary wet spot even if someone adds it — you may need a longer flashing or a different detail.

## Repair and retrofit cost

Adding one kick-out to an existing roof-wall joint, done right, requires pulling back a course or two of siding, carefully lifting the existing step flashing, installing the kick-out piece underneath and behind the housewrap, and resetting the siding. A roofer or siding contractor typically charges $150-$400 per location. Two or three locations on a single house is a few hundred dollars of work — a cheap credit to ask for during negotiation.

If the inspector or the contractor finds damaged sheathing or framing when the siding comes off, costs climb quickly. Replacing a couple of square feet of rotted OSB sheathing and a stud or two runs $500-$1,500. A larger section that has turned the wall framing to dust is a framing-plus-siding repair in the $2,000-$8,000 range, and that is before anyone looks at insulation and drywall on the interior.

## What to do if you're the buyer

Ask for the kick-outs to be installed before closing, or take a credit covering the retrofit plus a contingency for discovered damage. "Install three kick-outs, $900" is a reasonable ask. "Credit $1,500 at closing for kick-out retrofit and any discovered sheathing repair" is more defensive and usually accepted if the inspector's photos show any paint bubbling or staining below the joint.

## What to do if you're the seller

Retrofitting kick-outs before listing is cheap insurance against this becoming a line item during negotiation. If you suspect existing water damage, open one wall section first — it is better to know what you're selling than to have it surface during an inspection as "unknown damage behind siding, recommend further evaluation," which spooks buyers far more than a disclosed-and-fixed problem.

## DIY or hire?

If you can safely work on a ladder and remove and replace vinyl or fiber-cement siding without creating a new leak, this is a reasonable DIY. The flashing piece itself is $5-$15 at the hardware store, and cutting and bending it takes tin snips and a hand seamer. Most homeowners stop at the "remove siding without breaking the clips" step and hire a siding contractor — the right call unless you have done the work before.

## Bottom line

Kick-out flashing is a small, cheap part that prevents a large, expensive repair. "Missing kick-out flashing" on an inspection report should not kill a deal, but it should be addressed before the next heavy rain — either as a repair before closing or as a documented credit. The item worth watching for is not the flashing itself but what it has been letting through behind the siding for years.
