If you have ever noticed a haze, drops, or white mineral film trapped inside a double-pane window, you have seen a failed insulating glass unit. It is a common inspection finding, and it is more of a nuisance and efficiency issue than a hazard.

## What is happening

A double-pane window is two sheets of glass separated by a spacer and sealed at the edges. The gap is filled with dry air or an insulating gas such as argon, and a desiccant in the spacer absorbs any trace moisture.

Over time, the edge sealant breaks down from heat, sun, flexing, and age. Once the seal fails, humid air gets in and the desiccant is overwhelmed. The result:

- Fog or droplets between the panes, especially on humid or cool mornings.
- Permanent film or mineral spotting when moisture evaporates repeatedly.
- Reduced insulating performance, since the gas fill escapes.

Seals can fail anywhere from a few years to a few decades after installation, depending on quality, orientation, and sun exposure. South- and west-facing windows tend to be hit hardest.

## Is it a problem or just ugly?

It depends on what the inspector found.

- **Cosmetic and efficiency.** Foggy glass in an otherwise sound window is mostly about appearance and slightly worse insulation.
- **A sign of something else.** If the frame is rotted, leaking at the sill, or the window will not close, you may have a wider water problem. Check around the frame for soft wood, stains, and peeling paint. If you also feel air movement, see [drafty windows](/advice/drafty-windows).
- **Condensation indoors.** Fog on the room-side face of the glass is a humidity problem, not a seal failure. See [why is my house humid](/advice/why-is-my-house-humid).

A quick test: wipe each side. If the fog stays after you wipe the inside and outside, the problem is between the panes.

## Your options

1. **Do nothing.** Reasonable for a rarely used room or a small number of units, especially if you plan to replace windows eventually.
2. **Replace just the glass unit.** A glazier or window company measures the sealed unit, orders a new one, and swaps it into the existing frame. Works when the frame is good and the manufacturer's parts are still made or a custom unit can be built.
3. **Replace the whole window.** Better when frames are rotted, warped, single-pane-era, or the operating hardware is failing.
4. **Defogging services.** Short-term and results vary. Ask about guarantees.
5. **Check the warranty first.** Before paying for anything, find the brand and ask whether seal failure is covered.

## Typical costs

These are broad ranges and vary by window size, glass type, and local labor.

- Replacement glass unit (labor and material): often $150 to $500 per window.
- Full replacement window, installed: often $400 to $1,500 or more per window for standard sizes.
- Specialty shapes, large units, or tempered/laminated glass: higher.
- Defogging service: often $50 to $150 per window, with caveats above.

A handyman can sometimes handle simple swaps on small accessible windows, but a glazier or window company is better for glass units. Check labor against [handyman hourly rates](/costs/handyman-hourly-rate), and compare at least [three quotes](/advice/three-contractor-quotes) if you are doing more than a couple of windows.

## In a home sale

Foggy glass is a common line item on reports, often listed across many windows at once. It is usually not a deal-breaker, and a credit or a phased plan can be more practical than demanding every unit be replaced before closing. Look at it next to bigger items in [what must be fixed after a home inspection](/advice/home-inspection-what-must-be-fixed). The [sample inspection report](/resources/sample-home-inspection-report) shows how widespread seal failures are typically described.

Sellers can sometimes reduce the issue by replacing only the units most visible from the street or in key rooms, and by gathering warranty paperwork to hand to the buyer.
