A primary sump pump has one big weakness: it runs on household power. Storms that raise the water table also tend to knock out the electricity. That is the case for a backup system, and it is the reason inspectors often note "no battery backup" even when the pump itself works fine.

If you are still deciding whether your primary pump is healthy, start with [sump pump not working](/advice/sump-pump-not-working) and come back here for the backup question.

## Who benefits most

A backup is worth serious thought if:

- The basement is finished, or stores furniture, documents, or equipment you would hate to lose.
- Your area loses power during heavy storms.
- The pit fills quickly during storms or snow melt.
- You have a history of water in the basement, or the house sits low or in a high water table.
- The pump is older. Primary pumps often last roughly seven to ten years.
- You are away often. A backup works when nobody is home to bail.

If the basement is unfinished, dry, and holds little of value, a good primary pump, regular testing, and a plan may be enough.

## The main types of backup

**Battery backup pump.** A second pump installed in the same pit (usually at a slightly higher float level) powered by a 12-volt deep-cycle battery and a charger. If the primary fails or power drops, this one takes over.

- Pros: works with a private well, relatively simple.
- Cons: runtime is limited by the battery; the battery needs maintenance and eventual replacement.

**Water-powered backup.** Uses municipal water pressure to create suction that draws water out of the pit.

- Pros: no battery to maintain, can run long as pressure holds.
- Cons: requires city water, uses water while running, performance depends on pressure, and some jurisdictions restrict or regulate them. Not suitable for well water.

**Combination or standby systems.** Some units pair a battery pump with an alarm, a smart monitor, or a generator input. A whole-house standby generator also keeps the primary running, but costs far more and is a bigger project.

**A second AC pump on its own float.** Protects against primary pump failure but not against a power cut.

## What to check before buying

- **Pit size.** Two pumps and floats have to fit without tangling. Some pits are too small.
- **Discharge arrangement.** The backup needs its own discharge pipe or a proper tie-in with a check valve so it does not push water back through the primary.
- **Battery capacity.** Larger deep-cycle batteries give longer runtime. Check manufacturer charts for expected hours at a typical inflow.
- **Alarm.** A high-water alarm or notification gives you time to act.
- **Float quality.** A stuck float is a common failure point, and a backup with the same weakness is no help.
- **Electrical outlet.** The charger needs a protected outlet. If the outlet is on a [GFCI that trips](/advice/gfci-outlet-keeps-tripping), fix that first.

## Maintenance that keeps it reliable

- Test the primary and backup at least twice a year, and before storm season. Pour a bucket of water into the pit.
- Test the battery monthly using the unit's test function.
- Keep the battery in a cool, dry, ventilated spot. Heat shortens its life.
- Replace the battery on a schedule, commonly every three to five years.
- Clear debris from the pit and check that the discharge outlet is open, including in winter.

## Typical cost ranges

Prices depend on the brand, battery size, and local labor. These are rough ranges.

- DIY battery backup kit (pump, controller, charger), battery extra: often $300 to $700.
- Professionally installed battery backup system: often $600 to $1,500 or more.
- Water-powered backup installed: often $300 to $800, plus plumbing work.
- Replacement battery: often $100 to $300.
- New primary pump plus backup installed together: often $1,000 to $2,500.

## DIY or hire

A handy homeowner can install many kits: set the pump in the pit, connect the discharge with a check valve, mount the controller and battery, and plug in the charger. The risk is poor plumbing joints, an undersized discharge, or battery wiring mistakes. If you are unsure, a plumber can install it in a couple of hours.

## During a home sale

A missing backup is usually a "consider upgrading" note, not a defect, unless water history or an aging pump is also present. Buyers can ask for the pump's age and any water history.
