A frozen pipe is a $0 problem until it bursts, and then it's a $5,000–$50,000 problem. The prevention steps are cheap, and most of them take one afternoon in October. The trick is doing the right ones in the right order — homeowners routinely spend $80 on foam insulation for pipes that were never at risk while the one line in the garage that freezes every year gets nothing.

<QuickAnswer>
**Find the pipes that are actually at risk first:** exterior walls, crawl spaces, attics, unheated garages, and outdoor spigots. Then, in order of cost: **disconnect and drain hoses, insulate hose bibs ($3 each)**; **seal air leaks near pipes with foam ($10)**; **foam-sleeve exposed pipes ($1–$2 per foot)**; **add self-regulating heat cable ($30–$80 per run) on pipes in unheated spaces that have frozen before**. On nights below ~20°F: **drip the farthest cold faucet and open cabinet doors** on exterior walls. Keep the thermostat at 55°F minimum when away.
</QuickAnswer>

## Which pipes freeze (and which don't)

Interior pipes in heated rooms don't freeze. The ones that do:

| Location | Risk | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outdoor hose bibs with hose attached | **Very high** | Trapped water in the hose and spigot freezes and splits the pipe inside the wall |
| Pipes in unheated garages | **High** | Garage air tracks outdoor temperature within a few degrees |
| Crawl space and attic runs | **High** | Often uninsulated, exposed to vents and drafts |
| Pipes in exterior walls (kitchen sink, bathroom on an outside wall) | **Moderate–high** | Only a few inches of insulation between the pipe and outdoors |
| Basement pipes near foundation gaps or windows | **Moderate** | Cold-air infiltration at the rim joist |
| Interior-wall pipes | Low | Surrounded by heated air |

Walk the house once with this list. Every pipe on it gets one of the fixes below.

## The fixes, ranked by cost

**1. Disconnect hoses and cover hose bibs — $3 each, 5 minutes.** The single most common frozen-pipe failure is the outdoor spigot: water trapped in a still-connected hose freezes back into the pipe inside the wall. Disconnect every hose, drain it, and add a foam faucet cover. If the spigot has an interior shutoff, close it and open the outdoor spigot to drain. Frost-free hose bibs still need the hose disconnected to work.

**2. Seal the air leaks — $10, 30 minutes.** Cold air moving across a pipe freezes it far faster than still cold air. Look where pipes pass through the rim joist, foundation, or exterior wall and fill gaps with expanding foam or caulk. This is the fix most people skip and it's the highest-return one after hoses.

**3. Foam pipe sleeves — $1–$2 per foot.** Split foam insulation slides over exposed runs in crawl spaces, garages, and basements. Tape the seams. This slows heat loss — good enough for pipes in spaces that stay above the mid-20s. Not enough on its own for a pipe that's frozen before.

**4. Self-regulating heat cable — $30–$80 per run.** For the pipe that freezes every year, insulation alone won't do it. Self-regulating heat cable runs along the pipe (under the foam sleeve), plugs into a GFCI outlet, and warms itself only when it's cold. It's the definitive fix for garage lines, crawl-space runs, and mobile-home supply lines. See [our picks for insulation and heat cable](/tools/best-pipe-insulation-and-heat-cable).

**5. Heat the space — variable.** A crawl space with a closed foundation vent and a little house heat leaking in is often enough. For a detached or unheated garage with plumbing, a thermostat-controlled space heater set to 40°F is cheaper than a burst pipe — but only a model rated for unattended use, on its own circuit, away from anything combustible.

<SafetyWarning>
Old constant-wattage "heat tape" — the flat, non-self-regulating kind, especially if it's frayed, overlapped on itself, or more than 10 years old — is a documented fire cause. Replace it with self-regulating cable. Never wrap heat cable over itself, never install it under insulation unless the product is rated for it, and always plug it into a GFCI outlet. And never thaw a frozen pipe with a torch or open flame; that's how frozen-pipe calls become fire calls.
</SafetyWarning>

## On the cold nights themselves

When the forecast drops below about 20°F overnight:

- **Drip the farthest faucet** — a thin, steady stream of cold water from the fixture farthest from the main line, plus any fixture on an exterior wall. Moving water is much harder to freeze, and an open faucet gives expanding ice somewhere to push instead of splitting the pipe.
- **Open cabinet doors** under sinks on exterior walls so room air reaches the plumbing.
- **Keep the thermostat at 55°F or above**, including when you're away. A "vacation" setback to 50°F saves a few dollars and risks a five-figure claim. If the [heat goes out](/emergency-repairs/no-heat-in-freezing-weather), that's when the drips and cabinet doors become essential rather than optional.
- **Garage door stays closed** if there's plumbing in the garage.

## If you're leaving for more than a few days

Either keep the heat on at 55°F and have someone check the house, or shut off the main and drain the system: open the lowest faucet and the highest, flush toilets, and leave faucets open. An empty pipe cannot burst. A [smart water leak detector](/tools/best-water-leak-detectors) with a phone alert is cheap insurance for a house that sits empty in winter — it's the difference between a burst pipe running for an hour and running for a week.

<KensTake>
The homeowners who never have frozen pipes aren't lucky; they did a 20-minute walk-through in October. Hoses off, spigots covered, foam in the rim joist gaps, and heat cable on the one line that's frozen before. Total cost is usually under $100. The homeowners who call me in January with a wet basement did none of it and are about to spend the price of a used car on drywall and flooring.
</KensTake>
