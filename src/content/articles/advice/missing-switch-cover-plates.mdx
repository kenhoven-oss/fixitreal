Few inspection findings are as simple to fix, or as easy to dismiss, as a missing cover plate. The reason inspectors still list it under safety is easy to see once the plate is off: a switch box with its face exposed is an invitation to touch something live.

## What a cover plate actually does

A switch or receptacle sits inside a wall box. The plate that covers it:

- Keeps fingers and objects away from the screw terminals on the side of the device.
- Closes the gap between the device and the wall, which reduces dust, insect, and moisture entry.
- Helps contain sparks or heat if a connection fails inside the box.
- Gives a finished look that discourages casual tampering.

When the plate is missing entirely, you may see the device, the wire ends, or, worse, an open box with cut conductors and nothing inside.

## Three versions of the problem

1. **Plate missing, device intact.** The cheapest case. Fit a new plate.
2. **Plate and device missing, wires capped.** The box has live conductors under wire nuts. They need a blank cover and a proper box, with the circuit checked.
3. **Box exposed with loose or uncapped wires.** This is an electrician call and treat the circuit as live until proven otherwise.

Outbuildings, basements, and old garages produce versions two and three more often than living rooms, because plates get painted over, broken during projects, and never replaced.

## Do the quick check

- Walk through every room, garage, and outbuilding and look at each switch, outlet, and fan control.
- Note any with a missing, cracked, or loose plate.
- Look for discoloration, which can point to a bigger problem than an absent plate.
- For exterior outlets, confirm they have a hinged weatherproof cover that closes.

If you find one with exposed wire ends, turn off the breaker serving that area and stay away from it until it is fixed. If you do not know which breaker serves it, see our guide on how to [label a breaker panel](/advice/unlabeled-electrical-panel-fix).

## How to replace a plate

For an intact device:

1. Turn off the breaker, then confirm the device is dead with a non-contact voltage tester.
2. Remove the old plate screws, if any remain.
3. Match the new plate to the device type: single toggle, decorator, duplex outlet, GFCI, or blank.
4. Fit the plate, tighten the screws gently, and restore power.

Gently is the key. Over-tightening cracks plastic plates. If the device itself wobbles or its screw ears are crooked, adjust it in the box first. For anything involving loose conductors, call a pro. ## Cost

- Plates: roughly $1 to $5 each for standard plastic, more for metal or decorative designs.
- Weatherproof in-use outdoor covers: often $8 to $25 each.
- Handyman visit to replace several: often $75 to $200 depending on rate and number of plates. See [handyman hourly rates](/costs/handyman-hourly-rate).
- Electrician to cap, box, and make safe an exposed splice: often $100 to $300 as a service call. See [electrician service call cost](/costs/electrician-service-call).

## In a home sale

Most buyers and sellers can settle this one in an afternoon. A seller who replaces every plate before listing avoids a line item that makes the electrical system look neglected, and a buyer should treat it as a quick request, with the caveat that missing plates can point to unfinished work worth a closer look.
