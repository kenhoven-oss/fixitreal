A furnace that fires up, runs for two or three minutes, shuts down, and starts again ten minutes later is short cycling. It's the single most common furnace complaint in the first weeks of heating season, and most of the time the cause is airflow: the furnace is overheating because not enough air is moving across the heat exchanger, and its safety switch is shutting the burners off. The checks below go from free to expensive.

<QuickAnswer>
**Check the filter first** — a clogged filter causes most short cycling. Then: **registers and returns open and unblocked**, **thermostat location** (not in a draft or near a heat source), and **the flame sensor** (if the burners light and die within seconds, that's the sensor, not airflow). If those don't fix it, the high-limit switch, blower motor, or an oversized furnace are the likely culprits — **$75–$150 diagnostic, $80–$700 repair** depending on the part. Short cycling for weeks stresses the heat exchanger; don't let it ride.
</QuickAnswer>

## Two kinds of short cycling — tell them apart

The fix depends on *when* in the cycle the furnace quits:

| What you observe | What it means | Where to look |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Burners light, blower runs, everything shuts off after 2–5 minutes, restarts later | **Overheating.** The high-limit switch is tripping because the heat exchanger is too hot — almost always restricted airflow | Filter, registers, returns, blower |
| Burners light and go out within 5–10 seconds, then retry (often three times, then lockout) | **Flame sensing.** The furnace can't confirm the flame and shuts the gas off for safety | Flame sensor, igniter — see [furnace not igniting](/advice/furnace-not-igniting) |
| Furnace runs a normal cycle, but cycles start every few minutes and the house is comfortable | **Thermostat or sizing.** The thermostat is satisfying too quickly | Thermostat placement, oversized furnace |

## Checks in order, cheapest first

1. **Filter.** Pull it. If you can't see light through it, replace it and give the furnace a few cycles. A loaded filter starves the furnace of air, the heat exchanger overheats in minutes, the limit switch opens, and the cycle repeats. This one step fixes a large share of short-cycling calls. Keep spares — [the right filter](/tools/best-furnace-filters) matters less than changing it on time.
2. **Supply registers and return grilles.** Every register open, no furniture or rugs over returns, no closed-off rooms with the door shut and the register closed. Closing more than about 20% of the registers in a house restricts airflow enough to overheat the furnace.
3. **Thermostat location and setting.** A thermostat in direct sun, over a lamp, near a supply register, or in a drafty hallway satisfies or calls early. Check that it isn't set to a "cycle rate" or "swing" that's too tight (some smart thermostats have this in settings).
4. **Flame sensor** (for the light-and-die pattern). The sensor is a thin metal rod in the flame path; a film of oxidation stops it from confirming the flame. Power off, gentle cleaning with fine emery cloth or a dollar bill, reinstall. $80–$200 if you'd rather a tech did it.
5. **Blower and ductwork.** A weak blower motor, a slipping belt on older units, or a collapsed flex duct in the attic all restrict air. A blower that sounds strained or runs slower than it used to is on its way out: $300–$700.
6. **High-limit switch.** If airflow is verified good and the furnace still trips, the switch itself may be failing and opening early. $150–$300.
7. **Oversized furnace.** If a tech confirms airflow is fine, components test good, and the furnace heats the house in three minutes on a cold day, it's too big. Common after a replacement where the installer "went up a size." The only real fix is correct sizing at replacement time — see [furnace replacement cost](/costs/furnace-replacement-cost).

<SafetyWarning>
Repeated overheating is hard on the heat exchanger, and a cracked heat exchanger is the classic way a furnace produces carbon monoxide. Short cycling that has gone on for weeks is a reason to confirm every [CO detector](/tools/best-carbon-monoxide-detectors) in the house is working and to get the furnace looked at before the first deep cold. If the furnace [smells like burning](/advice/furnace-smells-like-burning) or soot appears around the burner access, stop running it and call.
</SafetyWarning>

## What it costs

- **Diagnostic visit:** $75–$150, usually credited toward the repair.
- **Filter:** $10–$30. **Flame sensor:** $80–$200. **High-limit switch:** $150–$300. **Thermostat:** $100–$350 installed. **Blower motor:** $300–$700. **Control board:** $300–$700.
- **Replacement** if the furnace is oversized or past 15 years with a cracked exchanger: $4,000–$8,000.

A tech who goes straight to "you need a new furnace" for a short-cycling complaint without checking the filter, airflow, and limit switch in front of you is skipping the cheap answers. Ask what the static pressure reading was and what the limit switch was doing; a good tech will have measured both.

<KensTake>
Short cycling is the furnace telling you it can't breathe. Ninety percent of the time the fix costs less than the diagnostic fee — a filter, an open register, a moved couch. Do those before you call, and if you still need the call, you've eliminated the obvious things so the tech starts from the real problem.
</KensTake>
