Winterizing a house is a short list of cheap jobs that prevent a short list of expensive ones. The order matters more than the thoroughness: a split outdoor faucet or a frozen garage supply line costs more than every other item on this page combined, so those come first. Everything below is ranked by how much damage it prevents per dollar and per hour, with the DIY and hired cost for each.

<QuickAnswer>
**Do these in order, before the first night near 28°F:** hoses off and foam covers on every outdoor faucet ($3 each) → sprinkler system shut off and blown out ($0–$150) → foam sleeves on pipes in the garage, crawl space, and exterior walls, heat cable on any line that has frozen before ($20–$80) → weatherstripping, door sweeps, and caulk where you feel drafts ($20–$40) → new furnace filter and a test run of the heat ($15) → gutters cleared and downspouts aimed away from the foundation → roof, chimney, and attic checked. **Occupied home, DIY: under $100 and one afternoon.** Add a plumber's drain-down ($150–$400) only if the house will sit without heat.
</QuickAnswer>

## What winterizing is actually protecting against

Four things break in winter, and they account for nearly all the cost:

| Failure | What it costs when it happens | What prevents it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outdoor faucet splits inside the wall | $300–$1,500 plus drywall | Hose off, cover on — 5 minutes |
| Supply line freezes and bursts in a garage, crawl space, or exterior wall | $5,000–$50,000 water damage; the most common winter insurance claim | Insulation, air sealing, heat cable |
| Furnace quits on the coldest night | Emergency service rate ($200–$400 before parts) and a cold house | Filter, test run, tune-up |
| Ice dam pushes water under the shingles | $1,000–$5,000 in ceiling and insulation repair | Clean gutters, even attic insulation, sealed attic air leaks |

Drafts and heat loss cost money too, but slowly — $10–$30 a month on the heating bill. They're worth fixing; they're just not the reason to start the checklist today.

## Disconnect hoses and protect outdoor faucets

**Cost: $3–$6 per faucet. Time: 5 minutes each.**

This is the first item because it's the most common failure and the cheapest fix. A hose left on a spigot traps water in the faucet body; that water freezes, expands, and splits the pipe a few inches inside the wall, where you won't see the leak until spring.

1. Remove every hose, drain it, and store it inside or in the garage.
2. If the faucet has an interior shutoff valve (look on the basement or crawl-space side of the wall), close it, then open the outdoor spigot to drain the section between them. Leave the spigot open for the winter.
3. Put a foam faucet cover over each spigot. They're $3–$6, and they do the job on frost-free hose bibs too — a frost-free bib only works if the hose is off.

If a faucet has split in a previous winter, ask a plumber to replace it with a frost-free hose bib ($150–$300 installed). The valve seat sits inside the heated wall, so there's nothing outside to freeze.

## Shut off and drain the sprinkler system

**Cost: $0 DIY with a compressor, $50–$150 hired. Time: 30–60 minutes.**

Irrigation lines are shallow, uninsulated, and full of water. Skipping this item cracks the backflow preventer ($200–$600 to replace) and splits lateral lines you'll find as wet spots in the lawn next summer.

1. Close the irrigation shutoff valve — usually in the basement or a valve box near the meter.
2. Open the test cocks and drain on the backflow preventer so the water inside it escapes.
3. Blow out each zone with compressed air. A homeowner compressor works if it can hold 40–60 psi at a decent volume; run one zone at a time from the controller until only mist comes out, and never exceed 80 psi or you'll damage the heads.
4. Set the controller to "off" or "rain mode" for the season.

If you don't own a compressor, a sprinkler company's fall blowout is $50–$150 and takes 20 minutes. It's the one hired item on this list that nearly everyone should just pay for.

## Protect pipes in unheated spaces

**Cost: $20–$80. Time: 1–2 hours.**

Interior pipes in heated rooms don't freeze. The ones that do run through garages, crawl spaces, attics, and exterior walls — and cold air *moving* across a pipe freezes it far faster than still cold air. The full method, ranked by cost, is in [how to keep pipes from freezing](/advice/how-to-keep-pipes-from-freezing); the short version:

- **Seal the air leaks first.** Where a pipe passes through the rim joist, foundation, or siding, fill the gap with expanding foam or caulk. This is the fix most people skip and the highest-return one after hoses.
- **Foam-sleeve exposed runs.** Split foam pipe insulation is $1–$2 a foot. Tape the seams. It slows heat loss enough for pipes in spaces that stay above the mid-20s.
- **Heat cable on the pipe that freezes every year.** Insulation doesn't add heat. Self-regulating heat cable ($30–$80 per run) under the foam sleeve, plugged into a GFCI outlet, is the definitive fix for a garage line or a crawl-space run with a history. See [our picks for pipe insulation and heat cable](/tools/best-pipe-insulation-and-heat-cable).
- **Close foundation vents** in the crawl space for the winter if they're the manual type, and keep the garage door shut if there's plumbing in the garage.

On the cold nights themselves — forecast below about 20°F — drip the cold tap farthest from the main, open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls, and keep the thermostat at 55°F or higher even when you're away.

## Seal drafts at doors, windows, and the rim joist

**Cost: $20–$60. Time: 1–2 hours.**

Air sealing is where the heating-bill savings are, and it also keeps cold air off the pipes you just insulated. Walk the house on a windy day with the back of your hand:

- **Door weatherstripping and sweeps.** If you can see daylight around an exterior door or feel air at the threshold, replace the weatherstripping ($8–$15 per door) and the door sweep ($10–$20). Both are a screwdriver job.
- **Windows.** Most window drafts come around the sash and trim, not through the glass: V-seal in the sash channels ($8–$15), caulk where the trim meets the wall ($6–$10 a tube), and a shrink-film kit ($6–$12) on windows you won't open until spring. The full method, including how to find the leak and when the window itself is the problem, is in [drafty windows: how to find the leak and stop it](/advice/drafty-windows).
- **Rim joist.** The band of framing that sits on top of the foundation, visible from the basement, is the leakiest part of most houses. Fill gaps at the sill plate and around every pipe, cable, and dryer vent with expanding foam ($8 a can).
- **Attic hatch.** Weatherstrip the hatch and lay a piece of rigid foam on top of it. Warm air going into the attic is what melts snow on the roof and builds ice dams.
- **Outlets and switches on exterior walls.** Foam gaskets behind the cover plates ($5 for a pack) are small but real.

A blower-door energy audit ($200–$500, often subsidized by the utility) finds the leaks you can't feel. Worth it on a house built before the 1990s; overkill on a newer one.

## Service the furnace and change the filter

**Cost: $15 DIY; $120–$220 for a tune-up. Time: 15 minutes, or a 60–90 minute service visit.**

The furnace gets tested in October, not on the first 10°F night when every HVAC company is booked for a week.

1. **Put in a fresh filter** and set a reminder for the next one — the real schedule depends on your filter and house, and [how often to change the furnace filter](/advice/how-often-to-change-air-filter) covers it. A dirty filter is the most common cause of a furnace that shuts itself off on a high-limit fault.
2. **Run the heat for 15 minutes** before you need it. Burning-dust smell for a few minutes is normal; a smell that persists, short cycling, or a furnace that lights and then quits is not — [furnace short cycling](/advice/furnace-short-cycling) and [furnace not igniting](/advice/furnace-not-igniting) cover both.
3. **Replace thermostat batteries** if it takes them. A dead thermostat on a weekend away is a frozen-pipe story.
4. **Book a tune-up** if the furnace is more than 10 years old, skipped service last year, or you heard anything new. A fall maintenance visit is $120–$220 and includes a combustion check, which matters on a gas furnace. The [HVAC service call cost guide](/costs/hvac-service-call-cost) shows what's a fair price.
5. **Test the CO alarm** on each level if you have gas heat. If there isn't one, a plug-in unit is $25–$40.

Heat pump? Same filter and test run. Clear leaves and debris from around the outdoor unit and don't cover it — it runs all winter.

## Clean gutters and check where water goes

**Cost: $0–$30 DIY; $120–$250 hired. Time: 1–3 hours.**

Gutters packed with leaves hold water that freezes, pulls the gutter off the fascia, and backs ice up under the shingles. Clean them after the last leaves drop, not before.

- Scoop the gutters and flush the downspouts with a hose; a clog at the downspout elbow is the usual blockage.
- Make sure each downspout discharges at least 4 feet from the foundation. A $10 extension or splash block is the difference between a dry basement and a wet one in the March thaw.
- Check that the gutters still slope toward the downspouts. Standing water in a level gutter is an ice problem waiting for December.

If the roof is two stories, steep, or you don't own a stable ladder, this is a job to pay for. [Gutter cleaning runs $120–$250](/costs/gutter-cleaning-cost) for most houses, and the fall is the visit that matters.

## Check the roof, chimney, and attic

**Cost: $0 to look; $150–$350 for a chimney sweep. Time: 30 minutes.**

- **Roof.** From the ground with binoculars, look for lifted or missing shingles, exposed nail heads, and flashing pulling away at the chimney, vents, and valleys. A small repair in October is $150–$400; the same spot after a winter of ice is a ceiling.
- **Chimney.** A wood-burning fireplace or stove gets swept before the season — creosote fires are a winter event. A sweep and inspection is $150–$350. Close the damper when the fireplace isn't in use, or add a chimney balloon ($40–$60) if the damper doesn't seal; an open flue is a 12-inch hole in the roof.
- **Attic.** Insulation should be even, with no bare spots and nothing pushed against the eave vents. Thin or disturbed insulation over the bathroom or the hatch is what causes the one melted stripe on the roof that becomes an ice dam. Baffles at the eaves keep the vents clear ($2–$3 each).
- **Sump pump.** Lift the float and confirm it runs. Winter thaws arrive fast, and [a sump pump that isn't working](/advice/sump-pump-not-working) is discovered during the flood. If the discharge line runs outside, make sure it can't freeze shut.

## Drain the plumbing if the house will sit unheated

**Cost: $20–$40 DIY; $150–$400 for a plumber. Time: 1–2 hours.**

Everything above assumes the house stays heated. A vacation home, a house between tenants or on the market, or any house where you can't guarantee 55°F all winter gets a different treatment: no water in the pipes means nothing can burst.

1. **Shut off the main water valve** and, if you have a well, the breaker to the well pump.
2. **Open every faucet**, starting on the top floor and working down, hot and cold. Leave them open.
3. **Flush every toilet** until the tank is empty. Sponge out what's left in the tank.
4. **Turn off the water heater** — gas to "vacation" or off, electric at the breaker — and drain it through the hose bib at the bottom. Draining an electric water heater with the power on destroys the elements.
5. **Open the lowest drain** in the system — usually a boiler drain or a hose bib near where the main enters the basement — and let the lines empty. Open the drain on the pressure tank if there's a well.
6. **Pour RV/plumbing antifreeze** (propylene glycol, pink, sold as "RV & marine antifreeze") into every sink, tub, and shower trap, the washing machine standpipe, and each toilet bowl and tank. Traps have to hold liquid to block sewer gas, so they can't just be emptied. One gallon does a typical house. Never use automotive antifreeze — it's toxic, and it's illegal to put into a plumbing system in most states.
7. **Set the thermostat** to 50–55°F if the heat will stay on at all, and have someone check the house weekly. If there will be no heat, also drain the boiler or any hydronic system — that's a plumber job.

A plumber charges $150–$400 for the drain-down and will blow the lines out with compressed air, which clears the low spots and loops that a gravity drain leaves full. If the house has a well, a boiler, or you're not sure where the low drain is, hire it. A [smart water leak detector](/tools/best-water-leak-detectors) with a phone alert is cheap insurance on any house that sits empty, drained or not.

<KensTake>
After enough Ohio winters the damage pattern is obvious: it's never the thing people worried about, it's the thing they forgot. The hose that stayed on the spigot. The garage line nobody insulated because it hadn't frozen *yet*. The furnace that was "fine" in September. Do the first three items on this list in October — hoses, sprinklers, exposed pipes — and you've handled 80% of the risk for under $50. The rest is about comfort and the heating bill, and you can do it over a few weekends.
</KensTake>
