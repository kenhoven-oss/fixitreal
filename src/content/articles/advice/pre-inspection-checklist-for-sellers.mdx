The best outcome from a home inspection is not a clean report. It's a report the buyer reads without losing confidence in the house. Those are different goals. A clean report happens by accident. A report that doesn't spook a buyer happens on purpose — because the seller spent a weekend before listing, walked the property with the same checklist an inspector uses, and knocked out every two-hundred-dollar finding that would otherwise show up as a deficiency.

This checklist is built from a real, anonymized [sample inspection report](/resources/sample-home-inspection-report) with 33 flagged deficiencies. At least half of them were cheap, obvious fixes the seller could have handled in a weekend. A buyer reading the actual report sees a long list and assumes hidden neglect. A pre-inspected seller never gives them the chance.

## The economics of pre-inspection work

A buyer's inspector is paid to find things. A professional inspector working in average residential stock will find 15-40 items on any occupied home, most of them small. The problem is how those items read in aggregate. One loose siding section looks like deferred maintenance. Ten deferred items across the report looks like a house that's been neglected for years. Buyers negotiate against the pattern, not the individual items.

Fixing $600 of maintenance before listing routinely saves $3,000-$8,000 at the negotiation table. The math is not subtle.

## The pre-list walk: exterior

Start outside and walk the property the way an inspector does, with a clipboard. From the sample report, the items a weekend outside would have erased:

- **Driveway and walks** — Any sunken area, lifted slab, or trip hazard, especially at an entry. The sample report's Comment 1 — *"Driveway has a sunken area at the connection point for the addition. This is a safety concern"* — is a mud-jacking job or a cold-patch fill, not a reason for the inspector to lead the report with a safety flag.
- **Side and rear doors** — Paint failure, rot, missing caulk at the jamb. Comment 2 in the sample: a garage side door with chipped paint and a rotted lower jamb. $40 of exterior paint and a short piece of PVC trim fixes the visible deficiency.
- **Siding** — Loose panels, popped nails, obviously detached sections. Comments 4 and 5 — *"Top section of vinyl siding ... is improperly attached"* and *"Several sections of vinyl siding are loose/improperly installed"* — are usually reseating clips and locking the panels back in. An afternoon.
- **Roof flashings and gutters** — Missing [kick-out flashing](/advice/missing-kick-out-flashing) is a buyer-fear item; so are gutters full of debris and growing moss. Comment 11 in the sample flagged exactly that. A gutter cleaning service runs $150-$300 (see [gutter cleaning cost](/costs/gutter-cleaning-cost)) and is cheap insurance.
- **HVAC compressor** — Clear brush, grass, and leaves from the unit and from within three feet of the coils. Comments 21, 22, 24 in the sample report all referenced plant debris around the HVAC compressors. A rake and ten minutes.
- **Frost-proof faucet** — Remove garden hoses before the inspection, especially the one that's been on all winter and may have corroded to the bib (Comment 31).

Total weekend budget for the exterior punch list: $150-$400 and six to eight hours.

## The pre-list walk: roof and attic

Most sellers shouldn't be on their own roof. Pay a roofer for a two-hour assessment — $150-$300 — and ask them to fix anything small while they're up there:

- Reset or re-nail loose ridge caps.
- Re-caulk exposed fasteners on vent boots.
- Clear moss and debris.
- Document the roof's remaining life so you have an answer when the buyer raises it.

In the attic, address what's visible from the hatch: insulation that has fallen out of place (sample Comment 14), a disconnected duct, visible staining. A disconnected-duct fix is a cheap handyman visit; buyers reading "insulation out of place" assume a bigger problem than there usually is.

## The pre-list walk: electrical

A homeowner should not open the panel. But you can:

- Test every GFCI with the test button and verify the receptacle resets.
- Test every smoke detector and CO detector with the test button, replace batteries, and replace any detector over ten years old.
- Walk the house with a $15 three-light outlet tester and note any that read a fault — Comments 18 and 19 in the sample report are both tester-detected faults that an electrician can resolve in one service call.
- Remove extension-cord workarounds. Comment 16 in the sample report flagged a tankless water heater powered by a drop cord. An electrician installing a proper exterior circuit is $200-$500; a buyer reading "extension cord powering an appliance" is a $5,000 renegotiation.

One electrician visit of two to three hours routinely clears four to six future deficiency items. Budget $300-$600.

## The pre-list walk: plumbing

Open every cabinet, look under every sink, run every faucet:

- Check for drips, corroded supply lines, wobbly P-traps. Comment 32 in the sample report flagged a kitchen sink trap that showed leak evidence. A $12 replacement trap and a wrench is thirty minutes.
- Confirm every sink drain stopper works — Comments 33 and 34 flagged missing or inoperative stoppers in bathrooms. $15 part each.
- Clear the water heater's access area if you have a tankless, and make sure the access door seals (Comment 28).
- Run the HVAC in cooling mode for twenty minutes and check that the condensate drain is actually draining and not routed uphill (Comment 6).

Plumbing weekend budget: under $100 in parts.

## The pre-list walk: interior

- Replace torn window screens (Comment 35 in sample: $15 each, five minutes each).
- Touch-up paint anywhere drywall patches show, ceiling stains have been repainted but faded, or corners show chipping.
- Clear the laundry room, washer-dryer area, and water-heater access so the inspector can actually inspect — Comments 36, 37 in the sample report were not findings about defects, they were findings about blocked access. "Unable to inspect" items raise more buyer suspicion than actual deficiencies.
- Tighten loose handrails, verify banister height meets a reasonable guard height.

## What to NOT bother fixing before listing

Some items will get flagged regardless and should be priced into the list price or disclosed up front:

- **Aging equipment** — A 2008 HVAC compressor will be noted as near end-of-life no matter what. Disclose the age; price it in.
- **Brick veneer cracks** — Hairline cracks will be flagged (see the [cracked brick veneer](/advice/cracked-brick-veneer) guide). Patching cosmetically before listing is fine; trying to hide anything structural is not.
- **Original roof at end of life** — If the roof is a sold-as-is item, disclose the age and offer an inspection-contingent credit toward replacement rather than trying to patch it cosmetically.
- **Known crawl-space or basement moisture** — Pretending it doesn't exist does not survive the inspection. Address it or disclose it.

## The stack: how to use this checklist

1. Book a pre-listing inspection before you list — $300-$600, half a day. The report tells you what the buyer's inspector will find.
2. Use the pre-list weekend to knock out everything under $200 and anything a handyman can clear in one visit.
3. Get written quotes on anything larger. Decide: fix, disclose and price-in, or leave for buyer-credit negotiation.
4. Keep receipts. "I just paid $450 to replace the drain trap and reseat the HVAC condensate line" is a stronger negotiating position than "we never noticed anything wrong."

## Bottom line

A buyer's inspection report is a snapshot of the house on one specific day. Sellers who treat the weekend before listing as a pre-inspection, not a cleaning sprint, control what that snapshot shows. Every flagged deficiency the inspector doesn't write up is a line the buyer never negotiates against. The cheapest dollars you spend in a home sale are the ones you spend before the sign goes in the yard.
