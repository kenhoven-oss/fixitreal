A single chirp every 30 to 60 seconds is not the alarm going off. It's the detector telling you one of two things: the battery is low, or the unit has reached the end of its life. Both are fixable in five minutes once you know which one you have — and the difference is printed on the back of the alarm.

<QuickAnswer>
**One chirp every 30–60 seconds = low battery or end of life.** Replace the battery first (yes, even on hardwired alarms — they have a backup battery). If it keeps chirping with a fresh battery: check the manufacture date on the back. **Smoke alarms expire 10 years from manufacture; CO and combination alarms 5–10 years** — an expired unit chirps until replaced, no matter what battery you put in. **Continuous loud beeping, not a chirp, is an alarm — get out and check for smoke.** Replacement is $15–$60 per unit DIY.
</QuickAnswer>

## Chirp, alarm, or something else

| Sound | Meaning | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single chirp every 30–60 seconds | Low battery, or end-of-life warning | Replace battery; if it continues, check the date and replace the unit |
| Three loud beeps, repeating, continuous | **Smoke alarm** | Get out, check for fire, call 911 if any doubt |
| Four loud beeps, pause, repeating | **Carbon monoxide alarm** on a combo unit | [Evacuate and call 911](/emergency-repairs/carbon-monoxide-alarm-going-off) |
| Chirp plus a spoken "low battery" or "replace alarm" | Voice-enabled units say which one it is | Follow the message |
| Chirping from a unit that seems to have no battery | Hardwired alarm with a hidden backup battery, or a sealed 10-year unit at end of life | Find the battery drawer; if sealed and expired, replace the unit |

## Fix it in this order

1. **Find which alarm is chirping.** With several alarms in a house, the chirp is hard to localize — it's a short high pitch that bounces off walls. Stand under each alarm for a full minute; the chirp interval is long enough that you can miss it. Interconnected hardwired alarms chirp only from the unit with the problem, not all of them.
2. **Replace the battery** — even in a hardwired alarm. Use the type on the label: most take a 9V alkaline; many newer units take AA or a specific lithium. A "new" battery from a drawer can be years old; check the date on the package.
3. **Reset it.** With the battery out (and, for hardwired units, the harness unplugged), hold the test button 15–20 seconds. This clears the low-battery flag that some units hold in memory. Reinstall the battery, reconnect, remount, press test once — you should get a full alarm, then silence.
4. **Check the date.** On the back of every alarm is a manufacture date. Smoke alarms are done at 10 years; CO sensors at 5–10 depending on model; sealed 10-year units at 10. An expired alarm chirps as an end-of-life warning and nothing you do to the battery will stop it. That's the [replacement](/costs/smoke-detector-replacement) signal.
5. **Hardwired and still chirping?** Occasionally the chirp is a wiring or power fault: a tripped breaker on the alarm circuit (the alarms run on battery and chirp about the missing AC), a loose harness, or a failed unit. Check the breaker first. If the alarm is under 10 years old and chirps on a good battery with AC present, replace the unit under warranty.

## Replacing it

Battery-only alarms: $15–$30 for a basic unit, $25–$60 for a sealed 10-year model that never needs a battery change. Hardwired: buy the same brand so the interconnect harness plugs in and the alarms keep talking to each other; if the brand is discontinued, replace them all as a set, because mixed brands don't interconnect. Our [smoke detector picks](/tools/best-smoke-detectors-for-homeowners) cover both, and [the cost guide](/costs/smoke-detector-replacement) covers what an electrician charges when you'd rather not.

One habit that ends the 2 AM chirp forever: when you replace one alarm's battery, replace them all. They were installed together, they'll die together.

<KensTake>
The alarms in most houses were installed the same week the house was built or last renovated, which means they all expire the same year. If one is chirping at end of life, the rest are within months of it. Check every date in the house while you're on the ladder, and replace as a set — sealed 10-year units, so you never do this again until the next decade.
</KensTake>
