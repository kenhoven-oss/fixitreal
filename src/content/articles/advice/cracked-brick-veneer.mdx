A crack in the brick on the outside of a house looks alarming. Most of the time it isn't — brick veneer is a decorative skin, not a structural wall, and it cracks because the veneer and the wall behind it move at slightly different rates with weather, moisture, and age. But a few cracks do matter, and reading the pattern correctly is the difference between a $400 mason visit and a $12,000 foundation job.

## Brick veneer is a skin, not a wall

On a typical modern US home, the brick you see from the sidewalk is one wythe thick — a single layer of brick tied back to a framed wall behind it with small metal brick ties. The framed wall carries the load. The brick is weather protection and curb appeal. That construction detail is what the Brick Industry Association's own technical notes confirm, and it is why most brick cracks are cosmetic: the veneer moving on its own doesn't threaten the structure behind it.

That is the first thing to know when an inspection report flags cracked brick, as the [sample inspection report](/resources/sample-home-inspection-report) on this site shows at the back corner of the house — a short vertical crack in the brick skirt around a crawl-space vent. The inspector's note reads, verbatim: *"Cracking is present in the brick veneer skirt located on the back south east corner of the residence. Unable to determine the cause of cracking at this level of inspection."* That hedge is standard. It means "we saw it, we don't know why, get a specialist."

## How to read the crack

The crack pattern tells you most of what you need to know before a specialist visit.

- **Hairline vertical crack, under 1/16 inch, no displacement.** Normal thermal and moisture movement. Caulk and forget.
- **Short stair-step crack in mortar at a corner or above a window, under 1/8 inch.** Old settlement, usually long finished. Worth monitoring with a dated photo, but rarely urgent.
- **Long stair-step crack across multiple courses, 1/4 inch or wider.** Active foundation or soil movement is possible. A structural engineer should look before a mason patches anything.
- **Horizontal crack along a mortar bed, especially mid-wall.** Can indicate failed brick ties or wall-tie corrosion. The veneer is detaching from the framing behind it. Not emergency, but not cosmetic either.
- **Displaced crack — one side pushed outward.** The wall is bowing. Call a structural engineer.
- **Diagonal crack originating from a foundation vent, window corner, or the ground up.** The ground has moved. Get an engineer.

Width matters more than length. A hairline that runs ten feet in a dead straight line is less worrying than a two-foot crack a quarter-inch wide with displacement. Dated photographs, taken a few months apart, answer the question almost everyone wants answered — is it getting worse? If the crack is the same width in two photos taken a season apart, it is stable.

## When the inside of the house tells you more than the brick

A brick crack that is also showing up inside — a drywall crack at the same corner, a door that recently started sticking, a floor that feels out of level — is a different conversation. The pattern says the framing behind the brick is moving too, which means the problem is below the brick. That is a foundation evaluation, and HUD's residential structural guide is explicit that visible foundation movement warrants a professional assessment, not a cosmetic patch.

The reverse is also useful: brick cracked on the outside, drywall and doors fine on the inside, is strong evidence the movement is limited to the veneer.

## What repair actually costs

For a cosmetic hairline, a tube of color-matched masonry crack sealer and a caulk gun from the hardware store is $15 and 20 minutes. It is weather protection, not structural work, and it is a reasonable DIY if you can match the mortar color.

For a mason to tuckpoint a short crack properly — grinding out the damaged mortar, repointing with a matched mix — plan on $300-$800 depending on access and how fussy the color match has to be.

Resetting displaced bricks, replacing a few failed wall ties, or installing a steel lintel above a cracked opening typically runs $1,500-$5,000. Add scaffolding and the number moves up fast.

If the mason's first visit turns into "you need a structural engineer," that evaluation is $400-$1,000 on its own, and the recommended fix — underpinning, piers, a drain system — is where the money goes. A pier or helical-pier foundation stabilization on a residential wall often starts around $3,000 and can climb past $15,000 for a whole side of a house.

## DIY or hire?

Hairline cosmetic sealing is DIY. Anything wider, longer than a couple of feet, stair-stepped, displaced, or reaching an opening is a mason at minimum and a structural engineer if the pattern suggests movement. Do not fill a wide or growing crack with caulk and call it done — you trap the symptom without addressing the cause, and the next inspector or the next buyer will raise it again.

## Bottom line

Cracked brick on a home inspection report sounds worse than it usually is. The report's job is to flag it; your job is to read the pattern, decide whether it's cosmetic or structural, and spend money accordingly. A photo and a short conversation with a mason answers most cases for a few hundred dollars. Only a small share of cracked-brick findings turn into foundation work — but those are the ones worth catching before closing on a house.
