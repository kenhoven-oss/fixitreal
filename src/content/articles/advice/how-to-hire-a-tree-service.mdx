<QuickAnswer>
Before anyone climbs a tree on your property: get a **certificate of insurance showing general liability and workers' compensation**, issued by the carrier, and **call the carrier to confirm it**. Get the scope **itemized** — removal from structure, trunk haul, brush, stump, cleanup — so three bids can be compared line by line. Pay **little or no deposit**; tree work has no materials to order. Prefer a company with an **ISA Certified Arborist** on staff, especially if you need an opinion on the trees still standing. And never let an uninvited storm-chaser start work the morning after.
</QuickAnswer>

After a storm put a tree on my garage roof two months ago, the removal quotes ranged from $4,000 to $18,000 — for the identical scope, remove from the roof and haul the debris. When the work is the same and the price varies 4.5×, the bidders aren't all the same kind of company, and sorting out who's real takes a specific set of questions. These are the ones that actually separate the companies.

## The five checks, in order

### 1. Insurance certificate — and a phone call

This is the one that matters most and the one homeowners skip most. Tree work has one of the highest fatality rates of any trade in the country. A climber falls, a limb swings, a ground worker gets hit by a dropped section — these happen every week somewhere. If the company doesn't carry **workers' compensation**, the injured worker's costs can come to you, the property owner, and to your homeowners policy. If they don't carry **general liability**, the section that goes through your roof instead of onto the lawn is your problem.

Ask for a **certificate of insurance (COI)** for both. It should come from the insurance carrier or agent — a one-page ACORD form with the carrier's name, policy numbers, limits, and expiration dates. Then call the agent listed on it and ask whether the policy is in force today. It takes three minutes. Lapsed and forged certificates are common enough in this trade that the call is not paranoid.

Reasonable minimums: $1 million general liability per occurrence, and workers' comp at statutory limits. Sole operators sometimes claim they're exempt from workers' comp because they have no employees — fine, but then who's on the ground while they're in the tree? If there's a second person, there's an employee.

### 2. Who's actually doing the work

Ask two questions: *Is there an ISA Certified Arborist on staff?* and *Who will be on site — your crew, or a subcontractor?*

An [ISA Certified Arborist](https://www.treesaregood.org/findanarborist) has passed a credentialing exam and keeps it current. Not every good removal crew has one, and for a straightforward "get it off the garage and haul it" job you don't strictly need one. But you want one if any of these apply: the tree is only partly down and someone has to decide whether the rest is safe; you have other large trees near the house that took the same wind; or the tree is near a property line and the neighbor's going to have opinions.

Subcontracting is common after a big storm and isn't disqualifying, but the insurance certificate needs to cover whoever is actually on your roof. If the company you hired is sending someone else's crew, get the sub's COI too.

### 3. An itemized scope

"Remove tree — $7,500" is not a quote. It's a number. You can't compare it to another number, your adjuster can't apply your policy's debris cap to it correctly, and you have no basis to dispute anything when the crew leaves a 30-foot trunk in the yard because "removal" meant "off the house."

Ask for these as separate lines:

- Removal from the structure (rigging or crane)
- Buck and haul the trunk
- Chip and haul the brush
- Stump grinding (or root-ball removal if it uprooted)
- Site cleanup and any lawn/driveway protection
- Crane, if one is proposed — and why

Now three quotes are comparable, and any line that's out of range on one of them is a specific question rather than a vague feeling. The [cost guide](/costs/storm-damage-tree-removal-cost) has fair ranges for each line.

### 4. Deposit and payment terms

Tree removal is a labor-and-equipment job. There are no materials to order, no permits with long lead times, no custom fabrication. There is no legitimate reason for a large deposit. A few hundred dollars to hold a scheduling slot is normal. A request for a third or half of the job up front is a red flag — and in some states it's illegal. California caps home-improvement deposits at 10% or $1,000, whichever is less; other states have their own limits.

Pay the balance when the job is done: tree gone, brush gone, stump ground if you paid for it, yard raked, and you've walked the property for the damage crews do on the way out (ruts, crushed shrubs, a nicked gutter). Get a written, signed invoice marked paid. If the removal is part of an insurance claim, that invoice is what the adjuster works from.

### 5. Local, findable, and referenced

A physical address you can look up. A phone number that's been in service more than a month. A state contractor registration where your state requires one (most don't license tree services specifically, but many require a general business registration or a home-improvement registration). Reviews with dates spread over years, not fifty in the last week.

After a storm, this is the check that filters storm-chasers. A company from three states away, working out of a motel, with a truck that says "Storm Recovery Specialists" and no address, can do fine work — but if they didn't, you will not find them in six weeks when the roof starts leaking where a limb went through.

## Red flags specific to tree work

- **Knocking on your door the morning after.** Real local companies are booked solid after a storm and don't need to canvass.
- **"We can start right now."** Usually paired with a price you'll regret. If your house is tarped and stable, the tree can wait for a competitive bid.
- **A crane on every quote.** Cranes are sometimes necessary. A company that proposes one for every job has a crane payment to make.
- **Pressure to sign an assignment of benefits.** This lets the contractor deal directly with your insurer and collect the payment. Decline. You pay them; your insurer pays you.
- **Spiking the tree.** Climbing spurs on a tree that's staying — for pruning — wound the bark. On a removal it doesn't matter. On the trees around it, it does.
- **No mention of your other trees.** A crew that takes the fallen one and never looks up at the leaning one next to it isn't thinking about your property. An arborist would.
- **Cash-only, no invoice.** You need a paid invoice for the claim, and for the day something goes wrong.

## The phone call, scripted

You can do most of the vetting in one call before anyone comes out:

> "I've got a tree down on [what it hit]. Before we schedule an estimate — can you email me a certificate of insurance for general liability and workers' comp? Do you have an ISA arborist on staff? Will it be your own crew? And when you quote it, I'd like it itemized: removal from the structure, hauling, brush, stump, and cleanup as separate lines. I'm getting three bids this week."

A real company answers all of that without friction, because they've been asked before. The ones who get defensive have told you what you needed to know.

## After the removal

Get an arborist to look at what's left. The storm that took one tree down put the same load on the ones next to it — root plates shift, limbs crack, and trees that survived this storm fail in the next one. A one-hour risk assessment ($150–$450) tells you which trees near the house need pruning, cabling, or removal on a calm day — at normal-season prices, not storm prices, and not through your roof.

And get a roofer up on the roof, even if the tree only brushed it. Small punctures from limbs are invisible from the ground and leak for months.
