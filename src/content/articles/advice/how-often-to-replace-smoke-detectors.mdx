Smoke detectors have a shelf life, and it has nothing to do with the battery. The sensor inside wears out. Every alarm has a manufacture date printed on the back, and ten years after that date it needs to come off the ceiling whether it still chirps on the test button or not.

<QuickAnswer>
**Replace every smoke alarm 10 years after its manufacture date** — the date is printed on the back of the unit, not the box. **Carbon monoxide alarms last 5–10 years depending on the model** (check the label); combination units follow the CO limit. Batteries in non-sealed alarms get replaced yearly; the alarm itself gets tested monthly. A house's alarms were usually installed together, so when one is expired, the rest are close behind. Replacement is [$15–$60 per unit DIY](/costs/smoke-detector-replacement).
</QuickAnswer>

## Why 10 years

Two sensor types are in use, and both degrade. A photoelectric alarm has a small chamber with a light source and a sensor; smoke scatters the light and trips the alarm. Over a decade, dust, cooking residue, and insects accumulate in that chamber and change how it reads. An ionization alarm uses a tiny radioactive source to keep a current flowing across a chamber; smoke interrupts the current. The source loses strength on a known curve, and the electronics drift with it.

The result isn't that the alarm suddenly dies at year ten. It's that response time gets slower and less predictable across the last few years, and you have no way to see it — the test button checks the horn and the circuit, not the sensor's sensitivity. NFPA sets the limit at 10 years because that's where the field-failure data says the sensor can no longer be trusted, and every major manufacturer now builds an end-of-life warning into the unit at that mark.

## Finding the date

Take the alarm off its mounting plate — a quarter-turn counterclockwise on nearly all of them — and look at the back. You'll find one of these:

| What's printed | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| "Manufactured" or "MFG" plus a date | The date the clock started. Add 10 years. |
| "Replace by" or "Expires" plus a date | The manufacturer did the math for you. |
| A date code like "2019-04" or "0419" stamped into the plastic | Year and month of manufacture. Add 10 years. |
| No date at all | Predates date labeling. Assume it's past 10 years and replace it. |

Write the replace-by date on the front edge of the alarm with a marker while it's in your hand. Future you, standing on a chair at 2 AM, will appreciate it.

## What an expired alarm does

Most alarms made in the last fifteen years have an end-of-life signal: a chirp every 30–60 seconds, sometimes with a different cadence or a spoken message on voice-enabled units, that continues no matter what battery you install. That's the single most common cause of "[it keeps chirping with a new battery](/advice/smoke-detector-chirping)." Older alarms don't warn you at all — they just quietly become less reliable, which is the worse outcome.

An expired alarm that still sounds on the test button is not a working alarm. The test button proves the horn and the battery are fine. It says nothing about whether the sensor will recognize smoke.

## Carbon monoxide and combination alarms

CO alarms use an electrochemical cell that is consumed over time, used or not. Rated life is shorter than a smoke alarm's — most models are 5 to 7 years, and some newer ones 10 — and it's printed on the unit. When the cell is done, the alarm chirps or shows an end-of-life code on the display.

A combination smoke/CO unit expires when the CO sensor does, which is usually before the smoke sensor would have. If you replace one, our [CO detector picks](/tools/best-carbon-monoxide-detectors) cover which ones are worth the money and where they belong.

## The replacement schedule that actually gets followed

- **Monthly:** press the test button on every alarm. You want a loud, full alarm, not a weak chirp.
- **Yearly:** replace the battery in every non-sealed alarm, including the backup battery in hardwired units. Daylight-saving weekend is the standard reminder.
- **At 10 years:** replace the alarm. Sealed 10-year units make the yearly battery step disappear, which is the main reason to buy them.

If you're standing on the ladder for one alarm, check the date on all of them. Houses get their alarms in a single batch — at construction, at a renovation, or at a sale — and they expire as a batch too. Replacing the whole set at once, with [the same brand](/advice/hardwired-vs-battery-smoke-detectors) so hardwired units stay interconnected, means one afternoon instead of six separate chirping nights over the next year. Our [smoke detector picks](/tools/best-smoke-detectors-for-homeowners) cover both battery and hardwired sets, and [the cost guide](/costs/smoke-detector-replacement) covers what to expect if you'd rather have an electrician do the hardwired ones.

<KensTake>
Nobody replaces an alarm that's still passing the test-button check, which is exactly why so many houses are running on twelve- and fifteen-year-old sensors. The date is the only honest signal, and it takes thirty seconds to read. Do it once, write the replace-by year on the edge of each unit, and you'll never have to think about whether the alarms are trustworthy again until the marker says so.
</KensTake>
