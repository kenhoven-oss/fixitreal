A drafty window is almost never a glass problem. The cold air is coming *around* the window — through gaps at the sash, the meeting rail, and the trim — and that is good news, because every one of those gaps has a fix under $15. Replacement is the right answer for maybe one drafty window in twenty. This page is about the other nineteen.

<QuickAnswer>
**Find the leak first** — on a cold, windy day, run an infrared thermometer or a lit incense stick around the sash and trim; the cold line or the bending smoke is the gap. Then fix in this order: **lock the window and make sure the lock pulls the sashes tight** ($0–$5) → **weatherstrip the sash channels and meeting rail** ($8–$15) → **caulk the trim inside and out** ($6–$10) → **shrink-film kit on windows you won't open until spring** ($6–$12 each) → **cellular shades or lined curtains to the sill** ($40–$150). Stop when the draft stops. **Replace only** for a rotted sash, a fogged failed seal, or single-pane glass in a cold climate — and price an interior storm insert ($100–$300) before a new window ($450–$1,200 installed).
</QuickAnswer>

## Where window drafts come from

| Leak point | How common | What it feels like | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sash to frame (side channels, bottom rail) | Very common | Cold air along the edges of the moving part | Weatherstripping |
| Meeting rail (where two sashes overlap on a double-hung) | Very common | Cold line across the middle of the window | Lock adjustment, V-seal |
| Frame to wall, behind the trim | Common, and the biggest leak when present | Cold air at the trim edge, often with a cold wall around it | Caulk, or foam behind the trim |
| Glazing (glass to sash, old wood windows) | Older houses | Cold at the edge of the glass, sometimes a rattle | Glazing compound or rope caulk |
| Weight pockets (old double-hungs with sash cords) | Pre-1950s houses | Cold from the sides of the frame even with everything sealed | Pocket insulation or a storm window |
| The glass itself | Everywhere, but it's not a draft | Cold to the touch, radiates cold, condensation | Film, shades, storm, replacement |

The last row is the one people confuse. Single-pane or failed double-pane glass feels cold and *radiates* cold — you feel it on your skin from a foot away — but it isn't moving air. The fixes for cold glass (film, shades, storms) and the fixes for air leaks (weatherstripping, caulk) are different, and most drafty windows need some of both.

## Find where the air is actually coming in

**Cost: $0–$30. Time: 10 minutes per window.**

Guessing is how people end up caulking a window that was leaking at the meeting rail. Do this on a cold day with some wind, with the heat running:

- **Back of the hand.** Crude but useful for the obvious ones. Move slowly along every edge.
- **Incense or a candle.** Hold the smoke an inch from the sash, the meeting rail, and the trim. Where it bends sideways or pulls toward the window, air is moving. This is the classic energy-auditor trick and costs nothing.
- **Infrared thermometer.** Point and read. A $20–$30 IR thermometer shows the trim at 66°F and the sash channel at 48°F, and that difference is the leak. It's also how you find the cold frame-to-wall gap behind the trim, which smoke misses because the air comes out along the whole edge rather than at one spot. If you own a few windows' worth of drafts, this is the tool to buy first.
- **Dollar bill test.** Close the window on a bill. If it slides out without resistance, the sash isn't sealing against the frame at that point.

Mark the leaks with painter's tape. Most windows have one or two, not six.

## Lock the window and tighten the sash

**Cost: $0–$5. Time: 5 minutes.**

On a double-hung window, the lock isn't just security — it pulls the top and bottom sashes together at the meeting rail and presses the bottom sash down onto the sill. An unlocked double-hung leaks at both. If the lock closes but doesn't pull the sashes tight, move the keeper (the half on the top sash) toward the inside by an eighth of an inch, or add a second lock on a wide window. Casement windows: crank fully closed and latch; a casement that's "almost" closed leaks along the whole hinge side.

This fixes more drafts than any product on this page and it's free.

## Weatherstrip the sash

**Cost: $8–$15 per window. Time: 20 minutes.**

Original weatherstripping flattens, cracks, or falls out, and most windows over 15 years old have none that still works.

- **V-seal (tension seal).** A folded strip of plastic or metal that springs open to fill the gap. Goes in the side channels of a double-hung and along the meeting rail. It's the right choice for sashes that slide, because it doesn't drag. Peel-and-stick, $8–$12 for enough to do two or three windows.
- **Foam tape.** For places where the sash *closes against* the frame rather than sliding — the bottom rail of a double-hung, the whole perimeter of a casement. Cheap and effective for a season or two; replace it when it compresses. Buy the closed-cell kind, not the open-cell craft-store foam.
- **Rope caulk.** A soft, removable putty cord you press into gaps by hand. The best fix for a window you won't open all winter: seal the whole sash-to-frame gap, peel it out in April. Also the fix for loose glazing on old wood sashes until you can re-glaze.

Clean the surface with alcohol first; adhesive strips fall off dusty paint by January.

## Caulk the trim, inside and out

**Cost: $6–$10 per tube; one tube does several windows. Time: 15 minutes per window.**

The frame-to-wall gap is often the single biggest leak, because the cavity behind the trim connects straight to the outside. Two beads:

- **Inside:** paintable latex caulk where the casing meets the wall and where the stool (the interior sill) meets the apron below it. Smooth with a wet finger.
- **Outside:** exterior-grade caulk where the window frame or brickmould meets the siding, and along the top of the drip cap. Don't caulk the weep holes at the bottom of a vinyl window — those drain water out of the frame and must stay open.

If you can see daylight or feel a strong draft at the trim and caulk doesn't stop it, the cavity behind the casing is empty. Pull the casing, fill the gap between the window frame and the framing with low-expansion window-and-door foam (never standard expanding foam, which can bow the frame and jam the sash), and reinstall the trim. Half an hour per window and the most permanent fix on this list. The [caulk and caulk gun guide](/tools/best-caulk-and-caulk-guns-for-bath-and-kitchen) covers what to buy.

## Add a shrink-film kit for the season

**Cost: $6–$12 per window. Time: 15 minutes.**

The clear plastic film kits work, and they work for a specific reason: the film is taped to the *trim*, all the way around, so it seals every gap at once — sash, meeting rail, and frame-to-wall — without you having to find them. The hair-dryer shrink pulls it tight enough that it's nearly invisible from a few feet away.

The trade-offs: the window can't be opened until you take it down, and the tape can pull paint off old trim (warm the tape with the hair dryer before peeling and it usually comes off clean). Use it on the windows you don't open in winter anyway — bedrooms on the north side, the ones behind furniture. It's also the only fix on this list that helps with cold glass as well as drafts, because of the trapped air layer. Buy the kit sized for your windows; the "patio door" size covers two normal windows for less than two kits.

## Cover the glass with thermal curtains or cellular shades

**Cost: $40–$150 per window. Time: 30 minutes to hang.**

This is the fix for cold glass once the air leaks are handled. A cellular (honeycomb) shade that fits snugly inside the frame, or a lined curtain that reaches the sill and sits close to the wall, traps a layer of still air against the glass. The room feels warmer immediately because you're no longer standing next to a cold radiant surface. Open them on sunny days on the south side — the free heat is worth more than the insulation during daylight.

Don't expect curtains to fix an air leak. If cold air is moving, it comes out under the curtain at the floor. Seal first, then cover.

## When the window is the problem

Everything above assumes the window is sound and just leaky. These are the signs it isn't — and what each actually costs to address:

| Problem | How to tell | Fix | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Failed glass seal | Permanent fog or streaks *between* the panes that won't wipe off | Replace the glass unit (IGU), not the window | $150–$400 per window |
| Rotted wood sash or sill | Soft when pressed with a screwdriver, paint bubbling, corners separating | Epoxy repair if small; sash replacement if not | $100–$300 repair; $250–$600 sash kit |
| Single-pane glass, cold climate | One layer of glass, often with a separate storm window | Interior storm insert, or exterior storm window | $100–$300 insert; $150–$400 exterior storm |
| Won't close, lock, or stay open | Sash drops, lock won't catch, balance broken | Balance or hardware repair | $75–$200 |
| Beyond repair | Several of the above on one window | Replacement | $450–$1,200 per window installed (vinyl); $800–$2,000+ wood or large units |

Two points worth knowing before you call a window company:

**A storm window is usually the smarter buy than replacement for a drafty-but-sound old window.** An interior insert — a glazed panel that press-fits into the frame from inside — brings a single-pane window close to double-pane performance for a fifth of the cost, keeps the original window, and comes out in spring. Exterior storms do the same from outside and stay up year-round.

**Replacement windows rarely pay for themselves on energy savings.** The Department of Energy's own guidance puts the payback at 20 years or more. People replace windows for comfort, for a sash that's falling apart, or because they're renovating anyway — all fine reasons. "To stop the draft" is not one of them until the $15 fixes above have failed, and they almost never do.

## If you rent

Everything removable works: shrink-film kits, rope caulk, a draft snake on the sill, cellular shades on a tension rod, and a second sash lock if the landlord allows a screw. Skip caulk and anything behind the trim. A window that won't close or lock is a repair request, not a draft problem — put it in writing.

A typical drafty window is fixed for **$15–$30 and under an hour**. A whole 12-window house done properly is a weekend and about $150, plus shades if you want them. That's less than the installed price of one replacement window — and it's the first item on the [whole-house winterizing checklist](/advice/how-to-winterize-a-house) after the plumbing.

<KensTake>
The window-replacement industry runs on the word "drafty." The salesman holds a lighter next to your old window, the flame bends, and you're signing for $14,000. The flame would bend a lot less after $12 of V-seal and a tube of caulk, and he knows it. Find the leak with a $25 thermometer, fix the gaps, and then decide whether the windows are actually bad. Most of the time they're fine — they just haven't been sealed since the house was built.
</KensTake>
