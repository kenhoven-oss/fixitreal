When something sparks, a basement floods, or a light needs rewiring, the person at the panel needs to know which breaker to switch off, and fast. An unlabeled panel makes that a guessing game. Inspectors note it because it slows electricians, wastes time in emergencies, and sometimes leads people to work on circuits they believed were off.

## Why it matters more than it sounds

- **Emergency response.** If an appliance is smoking or a cord is hot, finding the right breaker should take seconds.
- **Safer repairs.** Anyone replacing a fixture or outlet should shut off and verify the circuit first. A wrong guess is how people get shocked.
- **Clean troubleshooting.** Knowing which breaker covers which outlets helps a [tripping breaker](/advice/breaker-keeps-tripping) diagnosis go much faster.
- **Subpanels.** Detached garages and sheds often have a second panel with no directory at all, even when the main panel has one.

## What you need

- A helper, or a plug-in radio set loud and a long extension cord
- A plug-in outlet tester and a non-contact voltage tester
- Masking tape, a pen, and a notepad
- A printed or handwritten panel map to fill in as you go

## How to map circuits, step by step

Work with the panel cover (the outer door and the inner dead front) in place. You only touch the breaker toggles.

1. **Number the breakers.** Most panels already number slots. If not, number them yourself on the notepad, left to right and top to bottom, with the pair of positions that share a double-pole breaker marked together.
2. **Plug the radio or lamp into an outlet** in the first room. The outlet tester or non-contact voltage tester also works.
3. **Switch off one breaker.** Your helper checks outlets, lights, switches, and appliances in the house. Note everything that lost power.
4. **Switch it back on** before moving to the next.
5. **Repeat** for every breaker. It takes about an hour for a typical house.
6. **Check heavy-load circuits separately.** Furnace, water heater, range, dryer, and air conditioner usually have double-pole breakers. Confirm with a label on the appliance or a look at its dedicated wire.
7. **Mark unknown breakers as unknown.** An unidentified circuit may feed a junction box, a former bathroom, or an addition. Do not leave it to chance.

## Writing the directory

Use specific, plain descriptions:

- "Kitchen counter receptacles"
- "Upstairs bath lights and fan"
- "Furnace"
- "Garage lights and east wall outlets"

Avoid labels like "misc." or "plug." Include the room name and what the circuit actually serves. Write in pencil on the printed directory card if the panel has one, or use a label maker or typed sheet taped inside the door. Date it. If the house is later remodeled, update it.

## When to call an electrician

Call a licensed electrician if:

- The panel shows signs of overheating, scorch marks, a burning smell, or buzzing.
- You find a breaker that controls nothing you can identify, or a circuit that feeds odd places.
- You see double-tapped breakers (two wires on one terminal), melted insulation, or rust, which are separate defects.
- You have a second panel in an outbuilding that does not match what the main panel says feeds it.

An electrician can use a circuit tracer to map circuits without guessing, then produce a typed directory. Expect a service call or hourly rate. See [electrician service call costs](/costs/electrician-service-call) for typical charges.

## Cost summary

- DIY: a few dollars for tape and a label maker, plus about an hour or two of time.
- Electrician circuit tracing and labeling: often $100 to $300, depending on panel size and how many circuits cannot be identified.

## Where this sits in a repair negotiation

An unlabeled panel is a low-cost, low-drama item. In a repair request it sits near the bottom of the list unless it comes with other panel defects such as [no main disconnect](/advice/no-main-disconnect-electrical-panel) or [open knockouts](/advice/open-knockouts-electrical-panel).
