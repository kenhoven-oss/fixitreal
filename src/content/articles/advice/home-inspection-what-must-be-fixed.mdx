A home inspection report lists everything the inspector could reasonably find in three hours, from a stripped screw on a vent cover to a cracked foundation. Buyers read the report and panic. Sellers read the response and assume every item will become a demand. Neither picture is accurate. In most of the US, *nothing* on the report is legally mandatory to fix — but a narrow category of items will stop the sale in its tracks, and a wider category are reasonable to request. The art is knowing which is which before the counter-offer goes in.

This guide uses a real [sample home inspection report](/resources/sample-home-inspection-report) as the worked example. Thirty-eight comments — 33 tagged "Deficiency" and 5 tagged "Information" — and each deficiency deserves a different response.

## The legal baseline

The inspection report is not a contract. It is a professional opinion delivered to the person who paid for it. In most US states, the seller has no legal duty to fix items identified in the report. The buyer's purchase agreement usually contains an inspection contingency that lets the buyer request repairs or credits within a defined window; the seller can accept, counter, or refuse; the buyer can accept the response, counter back, or terminate the deal. That contingency is the entire mechanism. There is no third party ordering the seller to fix anything.

The two exceptions are:

- Items the buyer's lender requires complete before funding. These are not "mandatory" in a legal sense — the lender can refuse to lend, and the deal collapses, but no one is being compelled to fix anything.
- State-specific disclosure laws for certain hazards (lead paint, asbestos, radon, in some jurisdictions). These affect what sellers must disclose, not necessarily what they must repair.

Everything else is negotiation.

## Three buckets

Sorting an inspection report into three buckets saves the negotiation from becoming a line-item fight over everything the inspector flagged.

### Bucket 1 — Safety and lender-mandatory

These are the items a buyer cannot reasonably walk away from and the ones a lender may refuse to fund around. They move first and almost always get resolved before closing.

From the sample report:
- **Comment 17** — *"Circuit breaker 17 labeled nook is a AFCI type breaker. This breaker does not test by normal means. This is a safety hazard."* A non-functioning AFCI breaker on a modern panel is a code-required safety device that isn't working. Fix before closing.
- **Comment 16** — *"A drop cord is providing power to the unit. This drop cord is not rated for exterior weather exposure."* An extension cord powering a tankless water heater is a fire hazard, not a code-compliant electrical connection. Must be properly wired.
- **Comments 12, 13** — High moisture content and mildew/fungal growth in the crawl space subfloor. Active moisture in a structural area is a lender flag and a buyer-concern safety item (mold exposure, structural decay). Not optional.
- **Comment 1** — *"Driveway has a sunken area at the connection point for the addition. This is a safety concern."* The inspector called it a safety concern explicitly. Trip hazard at an entrance is actionable.
- **Comment 7** — Missing [kick-out flashing](/advice/missing-kick-out-flashing) on roof-wall intersections. Not a safety-of-occupants issue, but a water-intrusion item lenders care about and buyers won't accept as-is.
- **Comment 18** — Three outlets reading [open hot](/advice/open-hot-outlet). Loose wiring is a documented fire-risk pattern. Fix.

### Bucket 2 — Negotiate or take a credit

These are real items that reduce the home's value or will become expenses after move-in. A reasonable buyer requests a credit or a repair, a reasonable seller offers something, both sides land in the middle.

From the sample report:
- **Comment 3** — *"Cracking is present in the brick veneer skirt."* See the [cracked brick veneer](/advice/cracked-brick-veneer) guide. Request a structural engineer evaluation or a credit to cover one.
- **Comments 4, 5** — Loose and improperly attached vinyl siding on multiple sections. Water intrusion risk. Credit of several hundred dollars is standard.
- **Comments 6** — Condensation drain line exiting uphill. HVAC contractor visit, under $300.
- **Comments 8, 10, 11** — Missing roof granules, damaged ridge caps, fungal growth in gutters. These shorten the roof's remaining life. For a roof installed ten-plus years ago, this is a "price-in" item — a few thousand dollars toward the eventual replacement is reasonable.
- **Comments 21, 22, 24** — HVAC compressors blocked by foliage and debris. The repair is thirty minutes of landscaping, but the inspector's point is that reduced airflow shortens the compressor's life. Request the seller clear the units and inspect the HVAC.
- **Comments 32, 33, 34** — Kitchen sink drain leak, missing bathroom drain stoppers. Small plumbing fixes, under $200 each.
- **Comment 27** — Corroded HVAC overflow catch pan. $100-$300 part, plus a service call. Credit or repair.
- **Comment 38** — Crack radiating from a closet door frame. Often cosmetic settlement, but the inspector couldn't confirm. Credit for a drywall patch and monitoring, or a structural second opinion if paired with other settlement signs.

### Bucket 3 — Noise

Every report has items that aren't worth a line in the negotiation. Pushing on them makes the buyer look unreasonable and gives the seller an excuse to refuse the items in Bucket 2.

From the sample report:
- **Comment 2** — Rotted/missing paint on a side-garage door jamb. A seller can fix for under $100 or a buyer can do it in an afternoon.
- **Comment 9** — Exposed fasteners on attic vents. $20 of roofing caulk and ten minutes.
- **Comment 28, 31** — Water heater access door fastener, corroded garden hose on a frost-proof faucet. Trivial.
- **Comment 35** — Window screens with holes. Replacement screens are $15 each at the hardware store.
- **Comments 36, 37** — "Unable to inspect" items because of stored belongings. Not a defect at all — an access limitation. Noting it on a repair request accomplishes nothing.
- **Information comments** (20, 23, 26, 30) — The inspector's "Information" tags are data points, not deficiencies. Manufacturer dates on HVAC equipment and water heaters are useful context, not repair requests.

## The negotiation shape

A clean post-inspection counter-offer typically looks like:

1. Seller fixes Bucket 1 with licensed contractors, provides receipts, and verifies with a buyer walkthrough before closing.
2. Buyer takes a lump-sum credit at closing covering Bucket 2 items, usually 50-70% of the full repair estimate — because the buyer controls the work and can often do some of it themselves.
3. Bucket 3 is omitted entirely, or a token single item is included to show the buyer read the report.

Sellers who refuse everything and buyers who demand everything usually wind up renegotiating anyway. The three-bucket frame is how reasonable agents on both sides get to the same place faster.

## If you're the seller

Pre-inspect before you list — see the companion [pre-inspection checklist for sellers](/advice/pre-inspection-checklist-for-sellers). Fixing or disclosing items up front puts you in control of the narrative. Nothing drops a buyer's confidence faster than a long "Deficiency" list they weren't expecting.

## If you're the buyer

Read the whole report before responding. Bucket each item. Get at least one real contractor estimate on anything in Bucket 2 above a few hundred dollars — the seller's counter will take an actual estimate much more seriously than a buyer's guess. And resist the urge to ask for Bucket 3 items; they poison the negotiation for the items you actually care about.

## Bottom line

Nothing on an inspection report is legally mandatory to fix in most states. A narrow category of safety and lender-required items will close the deal if addressed and kill it if ignored. A middle category is legitimate negotiation territory. A large category is informational noise. The buyers and sellers who close cleanly are the ones who can tell the three apart before anyone starts writing counter-offers.
