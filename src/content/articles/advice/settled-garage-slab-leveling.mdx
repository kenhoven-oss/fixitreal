A garage floor that has dropped on one side, cracked across the middle, or stepped down at a seam is common and frustrating. The good news is that sunken slabs can often be lifted and leveled for less than the cost of tearing out and repouring. The key is figuring out why it moved.

## What is going on under the slab

A garage slab sits on compacted soil or gravel. If something washes that material away, shrinks it, or compresses it, a void forms and the concrete drops into it, a bit like a bridge losing support.

Typical causes:

- **Poor compaction at construction.** Backfill that was not tamped in layers settles for years.
- **Water erosion.** Downspouts, grading that slopes toward the garage, or a leaking pipe wash soil out from under the edge or through cracks.
- **Expansive or shrinking clay.** Seasonal moisture changes move the ground.
- **Tree roots.** They pull moisture from the soil and dry it out.
- **Frost heave and thaw.** In cold regions, repeated cycles shift sections unevenly.

## Settled vs. cracked vs. heaved

- **Settled.** Part of the slab has dropped. Often shows a step at a joint or a slope toward a low corner.
- **Cracked.** Hairline cracks are normal. Wide cracks, or cracks with one side higher, indicate movement.
- **Heaved.** The slab has moved up, often from frost or expanding soil.

An inspector will note it as a trip hazard, a drainage concern, or a possible structural sign, depending on how severe it is and where.

## Fix the cause first

Before paying for lifting, check:

1. Do downspouts discharge near the garage? Extend them well away.
2. Does grading slope toward the slab? Regrade so water flows away.
3. Is there a leaking water line, drain, or irrigation near the slab? Fix it first. If you suspect a hidden leak under concrete, see [slab leak warning signs](/advice/slab-leak-warning-signs).
4. Are tree roots involved?

Lifting a slab over wet, eroding soil is money wasted if the soil keeps washing out.

## Your repair options

**Mudjacking (slabjacking).** The contractor drills holes, about the size of a coin or larger, and pumps in a slurry of soil, cement, and water. The slurry fills voids and lifts the slab. It is heavy and typically cheaper. The holes are patched. Waiting time before use varies, so ask.

**Polyjacking.** The contractor drills smaller holes and injects expanding high-density polyurethane foam, which fills voids and raises the slab. The foam is lighter, and the floor is often usable the same day, but material cost is higher.

**Replacement.** Break out the slab, fix the base, and pour new concrete. It is the most expensive but may be right when the concrete is badly broken, thin, or the base is compromised.

**Do nothing but grind or patch.** Where a trip hazard is minor and the slab is stable, a concrete grinder or a patch compound can address a small lip. It does not restore the slope.

## Mudjacking vs. polyjacking at a glance

| Factor | Mudjacking | Polyjacking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Material | Cement-based slurry | Polyurethane foam |
| Weight added | Heavier | Light |
| Hole size | Larger | Smaller |
| Typical cost | Lower per square foot | Higher per square foot |
| Return to use | Often takes longer | Often same day |

Both methods work when done well. Ask the contractor how they will monitor the lift, how they handle the fill under the full area, and what the warranty covers.

## Typical cost ranges

These are rough, regional, and depend on the size of the area and access.

- Mudjacking: often $3 to $7 per square foot, with minimum job charges common.
- Polyjacking: often $5 to $25 per square foot.
- A typical single-section garage repair: often $500 to $3,000, rising for full two-car slabs.
- Full replacement of a two-car garage slab: often $4,000 to $10,000 or more.

## When to call a structural engineer

Bring one in if:

- The settlement is next to the foundation wall or the garage is attached and walls or door frames are cracking.
- Settlement is continuing or accelerating.
- The slab has large voids, or the soil beneath is suspect.
- You are buying and the seller disputes the cause.

An engineer's report gives a neutral answer and helps in negotiations.
